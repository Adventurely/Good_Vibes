## Morphology

**Habit.** Acaulescent (effectively stemless) evergreen perennial herb forming a flat, near-horizontal rosette. Leaves radiate in a tight spiral (divergence approx. 137.5 deg) from an extremely compressed, thick, succulent stem 1.0-2.5 cm in diameter, and because the internodes are only 0.3-0.8 cm the rosette reads as 3-5 shingled tiers rather than a spiral - outer/older tiers sit lower and their blades pass UNDER the inner tiers, so the plant is a layered plate, not a fan. Mature standard rosette 20-30 cm across (large standards to 40-45 cm; semi-miniatures 15-20 cm; miniatures 8-15 cm) and only 8-15 cm tall soil to canopy. As lower leaves abscise the compressed stem elongates into a bare, scarred 'neck' 0-6 cm long bearing elliptical leaf scars at the phyllotactic interval; growers periodically repot deeper to bury it. Roots are fine, fibrous, white and shallow, confined largely to the top 5-8 cm - hence shallow pots. Axillary buds can break as suckers, producing secondary crowns that break the rosette's symmetry. In habitat (shaded, seepage-wet gneiss faces and forest floor in the Eastern Arc Mountains of Tanzania and the Taita Hills of Kenya) it grows as a lithophyte or terrestrial in permanently humid, deeply shaded cloud forest - the flat plate is a light-harvesting posture for deep shade, and every deviation from flat is a stress signal.

**Leaf.** Simple, entire, ovate to broadly ovate or nearly orbicular, with a cordate (heart-shaped, notched) base and a rounded to obtuse apex. Blade 2.5-5.5 cm long in the wild species, 5-8 cm in standard cultivars, 1.5-3 cm in miniatures; width 0.80-1.00 of the length. Blade is thick and succulent for its size (0.8-1.5 mm) and BRITTLE - it snaps rather than bends. Margin entire to crenate or shallowly scalloped, typically 10-30 scallops per margin at 0.5-3 mm amplitude; some cultivars are wavy or serrate. Venation pinnate, with the primary and secondary veins SUNKEN on the upper surface (up to 2 mm) and raised beneath, giving the quilted, puffed-interveinal-cushion look of many cultivars; the wild species is flatter. Both surfaces, plus the petiole, peduncle, pedicel and calyx, are densely covered in short white multicellular trichomes 0.3-1.5 mm long at roughly 15-70 per mm2, standing 20-60 degrees off the surface - this is the velvet, and it dominates the plant's appearance: it retro-reflects at grazing angles so leaf edges look PALER than leaf centres, it kills specular gloss, and it makes the surface superhydrophobic so water sits as near-spherical beads on the hair tips for hours. Upper surface mid to deep green (approx. #4A6B2E healthy); underside pale grey-green to strongly red-purple with anthocyanin, cultivar-dependent and deepening under high light or cold. Petiole fleshy, brittle, hairy, semi-terete with a shallow groove on top, 2-10 cm long and 3-7 mm thick, functioning as a hydrostat - it is the petiole, not the blade, that sags when turgor is lost. Cultivar foliage types worth encoding: 'boy' foliage is plain green; 'girl' foliage carries a distinct cream-to-white blotch at the blade base around the petiole junction; variegated forms carry white or pink marginal or mosaic variegation that intensifies in cool conditions.

**Flower.** Zygomorphic (bilaterally symmetric - NOT radial; modelling it radially is the single most common flower error). Borne in axillary cymes: a slender, hairy peduncle 4-12 cm long and 1.5-3 mm thick emerges from a LEAF AXIL - typically in the second tier, not from the crown centre - and threads upward between the petioles, carrying 2-8 flowers on pedicels 1-3 cm long. The cyme holds the flowers 1-3 cm above or nestled just at the leaf canopy. Calyx of 5 free, narrow, lanceolate, hairy green sepals 4-7 mm long. Corolla sympetalous with a short tube 4-8 mm opening into 5 spreading lobes in a 2+3 arrangement: the 2 upper lobes are smaller (8-15 mm) and swept slightly back, the 3 lower lobes larger (12-25 mm) and forward-facing - the flower has a distinct top and bottom. Species colour is violet-blue to blue-purple (roughly #6B4FA8 to #4B3C8F) with a paler, sometimes white throat; the epithet ionantha means 'violet-flowered'. Petals are thin (0.2-0.4 mm), matte and micro-papillate rather than glossy, and strongly translucent - they glow when backlit and show a fine vein tracery. Androecium: 2 fertile stamens with short curved filaments and conspicuous bright yellow anthers 2.5-4 mm, held face-to-face as a forward-pointing beak at the throat, dehiscing by APICAL PORES (poricidal - buzz-pollinated by vibrating bees in habitat); 3 staminodes vestigial and hidden. That paired yellow beak is the flower's read-at-a-glance identity. Gynoecium: superior ovary, a single hairy style 8-15 mm curving upward and to one side so the small stigma sits offset from the anther beak. Fruit (essentially never formed indoors, since nothing buzz-pollinates a houseplant): a straight oblong capsule 5-15 mm long containing hundreds of dust-like seeds 0.3-0.5 mm - note the capsule is NOT spirally twisted, unlike the subgenus Streptocarpus proper. Day-neutral: flowers continuously in overlapping flushes given adequate light, 8-10 months a year. Individual corolla lasts 2-3 weeks; a cyme opens sequentially over 4-6 weeks, so open flowers, spent flowers, and unopened buds always coexist on one stalk.

## Care

**Light.** Bright INDIRECT light only. Target 8,000-16,000 lux (750-1,500 fc) at leaf height for 12-14 h/day; DLI 4-6 mol/m2/day. East or north window, or behind a sheer curtain in a south/west window. Under artificial light: LED/fluorescent 25-40 cm above the rosette, 12-14 h on a timer; rotate the pot 90 degrees every 2-3 days or the rosette grows lopsided toward the source. Sustained direct midday sun above ~25,000 lux bleaches within days. Below ~5,000 lux the plant survives but stops flowering entirely and etiolates. Photoperiod is NOT a bloom trigger - this is a day-neutral species; total daily light integral gates flowering.

**Water.** Keep the mix evenly moist and never sodden or bone dry - in habitat the roots sit in constantly seeping, well-aerated rock crevices. Water when the top 1 cm reads dry to the touch and the pot has lost noticeable weight; typically every 4-7 days in a 9 cm pot at 21 C and 50% RH. Water TEPID (18-24 C, within 2-3 C of leaf temperature) from below into the saucer or via a wick, let it draw 20-30 minutes, then TIP OFF any water still standing. Never let the pot sit in water for hours. Never pour into the crown, and keep droplets off the foliage. Use rainwater, distilled, or tap water left to stand; never artificially softened water (sodium accumulates and burns the roots).

**Temperature.** 18-24 C day, 16-21 C night; a 3-5 C night drop is fine and slightly improves flower substance. Below 16 C growth slows and bloom stops; below 13 C is chilling injury; below 7 C causes water-soaked tissue collapse within hours. Above 27 C buds abort and colours wash out; above 30 C flowering stops entirely and the crown tightens. Steady is better than optimal - the plant tolerates a constant 17 C far better than a 24 C day with a 10 C draft off a winter windowpane. Keep foliage from touching cold glass.

**Humidity.** 50-70% RH ideal, 40% tolerable, below 35% causes bud blast. Raise humidity with a pebble tray or grouped plants, NEVER by misting: the leaf trichomes hold droplets as standing beads for hours, which invites Botrytis and, if the water is cool, ring spot. Humidity above ~80% with still air causes powdery mildew and crown rot, so ventilation matters as much as the number.

**Soil.** Light, sterile, high-porosity soilless mix - roughly equal parts peat or coir, coarse perlite, and vermiculite (1:1:1). Bulk density must be low; standard bagged potting soil is too dense and holds too much water. pH 6.0-6.5 slightly acidic (above 7.0 locks out iron and the youngest leaves go pale between the veins). Low salinity: feed a balanced soluble fertiliser (e.g. 20-20-20) at one-quarter label strength with every watering rather than full strength monthly, and leach the pot with plain tepid water monthly. Shallow, wide 'azalea' pot; pot diameter about one third of the leaf span (8-10 cm pot for a 25-30 cm rosette); repot every 6-12 months into fresh mix at the SAME pot size, setting the plant deeper to bury the accumulated neck.

### What a real grower knows

Grower knowledge a naive implementation gets wrong:

1. TAXONOMY. Since 2012 the accepted name is Streptocarpus ionanthus (H.Wendl.) Christenh.; Saintpaulia ionantha is a synonym kept in horticulture. Wild populations in the Eastern Arc Mountains (Nguru, East/West Usambara, Tanzania; Taita Hills, Kenya) are IUCN-threatened by forest clearance. The wild species is a lithophyte on shaded, seepage-wet gneiss and forest floor under closed canopy - permanently humid, permanently dim, never dry, never in direct sun. Every care rule below is that habitat restated.

2. ALMOST NOTHING SOLD IS THE SPECIES. Retail 'African violets' are complex hybrids (largely S. ionanthus x S. confusus x others) with bigger, often doubled or ruffled corollas, quilted or variegated leaves. If the game labels the specimen S. ionantha, model the species: ~2-2.5 cm single violet-blue flowers, thinner and flatter leaves, looser rosette. Giving a labelled species plant a 5 cm ruffled pink double is a botanical bug.

3. WATER TEMPERATURE IS A SEPARATE VARIABLE FROM WATER AMOUNT. Room-temperature water (18-24 C, within ~2-3 C of the leaf) is mandatory. Water more than ~8 C below leaf temperature landing on a blade causes permanent chlorotic ring spot in 24-72 h. This is a cell-chilling injury, not a sunburn-through-droplet-lens effect. Model water temperature as its own player-visible property.

4. WATER FROM BELOW OR AT THE SOIL SURFACE, NEVER OVER THE CROWN. Water standing in the crown at night rots it. Saucer/wick watering: fill, let the mix draw for 20-30 min, then POUR OFF the remainder. Standing water for hours is the single fastest route to root rot.

5. LEAVES ARE BRITTLE, NOT FLOPPY. Fleshy petioles snap cleanly rather than bend. Handling, brushing past, or a knocked pot removes leaves permanently, leaving a stub scar on the crown. This should be a real physical consequence in-game.

6. POT-RIM SALT BURN. Constant dilute feeding leaves a white crystalline crust on the rim; petioles resting on that crust develop a brown corky constriction at exactly the contact line and the leaf beyond it dies while the rest of the plant looks perfect. Real growers wax the rim, use foil collars, or leach the pot monthly with 4x its volume of plain tepid water.

7. UNDERPOTTING IS CORRECT. Roots are fine, fibrous and shallow, occupying the top 5-8 cm. Pot diameter should be about ONE THIRD of the leaf span (a 25 cm rosette wants an 8-9 cm shallow/azalea pot). Repotting into a bigger pot causes rot and stops bloom - counter-intuitive and a great trap mechanic.

8. THE NECK. As lower leaves are shed the compressed stem elongates into a bare, scarred 'neck'. Growers fix this by repotting deeper (burying the neck up to the lowest leaves) roughly every 6-12 months; the buried neck roots. Neck length is a running record of the plant's history and should be persistent state.

9. SINGLE CROWN. Suckers (axillary side rosettes) must be pinched out; a multi-crowned plant loses its symmetry and its bloom.

10. GROOMING DRIVES BLOOM. Deadheading spent cymes and removing the oldest tier of leaves both increase flowering. An ungroomed plant is not just untidy, it flowers measurably less.

11. NO DORMANCY, NO SEASON. Given stable conditions it blooms 8-10 months a year in overlapping flushes. Reduced winter bloom in cultivation is a light and temperature artefact, not an endogenous rhythm. Do not build a dormant phase.

12. BUZZ POLLINATION. The two fused yellow anthers open by apical pores; in the wild only vibrating bees release the pollen. Nothing indoors does, so capsules essentially never form on a houseplant - correct behaviour is flowers that simply abscise. Thrips damage looks like an anther emptied of pollen with brown streaks smeared across the lower petals.

13. CYCLAMEN MITE is the signature pest and is nearly invisible; you diagnose it by geometry (a stunted, hairy, grey knot at the crown) rather than by seeing the animal.

## Decline

### Underwatering / soil dried out completely

Turgor loss propagates outside-in. The OLDEST outer tier goes first: the fleshy petiole sags along its length into a shallow catenary so the blade tips drop 3-6 cm and drape over the pot rim, blade held roughly flat but now tilted 30-50 degrees below horizontal; blade margins roll gently DOWNWARD and inward maybe 10-15 degrees. The velvet sheen goes matte and the colour shifts from mid-green toward grey-olive as the collapsed trichome layer scatters less light. The crown and the inner tier stay upright and level - this outside-in gradient is the diagnostic silhouette. Soil surface renders pale, shrunken, and pulled 2-4 mm away from the pot wall. Buds shrivel from the tip and drop, leaving bare green pedicel stubs on the peduncle; open flowers go papery at the lobe margins and abscise.

*Onset:* First visible sag 2-4 days after the mix goes fully dry; full outer-tier drape by day 4-6; irreversible marginal crisping from day 7-10; death 2-4 weeks (this species survives a dry-out far longer than it looks like it will)  
*Reversible:* Yes, and dramatically - full turgor returns within 2-12 h of a tepid bottom-water, which makes it excellent player feedback. But any leaf that reached the crisp stage keeps a permanent 1-3 mm brown papery band along the margin, tip inward, that never re-greens. Repeated cycles shorten outer-leaf life: those leaves yellow and abscise weeks early, leaving stub scars and a longer bare neck. Blasted buds never reopen; that cyme is lost.

### Overwatering / waterlogged mix / crown rot (Pythium, Phytophthora); accelerated by cold soil, a too-large pot, dense soil, or water poured into the crown

Geometrically almost identical drooping to drought - AND THAT IS THE POINT, the player must read the context, not the leaves. Differentiators: the soil surface renders dark, glossy-wet, often with a green algal film or moss and fungus gnats hovering; the pot is heavy. Then the specific signs appear: petioles turn translucent and water-soaked at the base where they meet the crown, darkening to brown then black over a 5-15 mm band; the whole leaf beyond that band goes limp all at once rather than sagging gradually, and hangs straight down against the pot. The crown centre greys and turns mushy, the youngest leaves flatten and collapse inward like a folding umbrella, and the rosette's 3-tier plate structure disintegrates into a flat splayed star. Terminal stage: the plant lifts free of the mix with a black stub and no white roots; healthy roots are fine, white, and thread-like, rotted ones are brown, slimy, and slough off leaving bare thread cores.

*Onset:* 5-14 days of sodden mix at 21 C; 3-7 days if the mix is also below 16 C. Crown blackening once started progresses to total collapse in 4-8 days  
*Reversible:* Only in the earliest window. If discoloration is confined to one or two outer petiole bases, the plant can be unpotted, rotted roots cut away, and rerooted in fresh barely-damp mix - it survives roughly half the time and permanently loses its lower tier. Once the CROWN itself greys or softens, it is unrecoverable; the only salvage is taking an undamaged outer leaf as a cutting, which takes 8-12 weeks to make a new plantlet. Model this as the species' primary death mode.

### Cold water on the foliage - chilling injury to palisade cells when water more than ~8 C below leaf temperature sits on the blade. Also produced by a cold draft on wet leaves

Sharply defined pale marks that exactly record where droplets sat. Each is a pale yellow to cream to tan ring, arc, crescent, or splash 3-15 mm across, often a bright outline with near-normal green enclosed - a droplet's rim. Edges are hard, not feathered. No swelling, no sunkenness, no bending: the leaf geometry is completely unchanged, only albedo. Marks cluster on the upper, most exposed leaves and along the line a watering can travelled. Under specular light the affected patch is slightly duller than surrounding velvet because the trichomes over dead cells collapse.

*Onset:* 24-72 h; classically visible on day 2. Does not spread after that  
*Reversible:* NO. The chlorophyll in those cells is destroyed and the tissue never re-greens. The mark persists for the remaining life of that leaf (months to over a year) and the only remedy is removing the leaf, which costs the plant a tier and lengthens the neck. This is the game's signature 'careless click, permanent scar' mechanic - it should be cheap to cause and impossible to undo.

### Direct sun / excessive light intensity (above ~25,000 lux, especially unfiltered midday sun through glass)

Two-part response. (a) Bleaching: the leaves nearest the light source lose saturation from deep green to yellow-green to a washed bone-tan, brightest in the interveinal panels while the veins stay a green tracery for a while; the change is a directional gradient across the rosette, strongest on the sun side. (b) Shading posture: petioles SHORTEN and bend downward so the whole rosette cups over the pot rim like a closing hand, blades curling under at the margins by 20-40 degrees and the rosette diameter visibly contracting 15-25%. Acute scorch adds dry, silvery-tan, papery patches with a sharp boundary against green, sunken slightly and no longer velvet. Underside anthocyanin deepens toward red-purple. Flowers fade fast - purple corollas go washed grey-lilac and last 3-4 days instead of 2-3 weeks.

*Onset:* Acute papery scorch in 1-2 days of harsh midday exposure; progressive bleaching and cupping over 3-10 days; rosette contraction obvious by day 7-14  
*Reversible:* Partly. Move it to correct light and posture relaxes over 1-2 weeks and colour in mildly bleached tissue recovers over 3-6 weeks. Severely bleached leaves stay pale for life, and the papery scorched patches are dead tissue - permanent. Full cosmetic recovery requires growing out 2-3 new tiers, which is 2-4 months in real time.

### Insufficient light (below ~5,000 lux, or short daily exposure)

Etiolation - the rosette turns from a flat plate into a funnel. Petioles elongate 1.5-2.5x (a 4 cm petiole becomes 7-10 cm) and lift from horizontal to 30-60 degrees above horizontal, all leaves reaching upward and inward so the silhouette becomes a shuttlecock or badly-made bouquet. Blades enlarge, thin, and darken to a deeper green with a slightly higher gloss and lower trichome density. Internodes at the crown stretch from ~0.4 cm to ~1 cm so tiers separate visibly and the bare scarred neck lengthens. Flowering stops completely: no new peduncles emerge from the axils, and the existing cyme finishes and drops, leaving the plant entirely green. Leaf colour on the light side stays normal while the dark side pales - a lopsided rosette if the pot is never rotated.

*Onset:* Petiole elongation measurable at 10-14 days, unmistakable by 3-4 weeks; bloom cessation is the first sign and shows within 2-3 weeks as the current flush ends with nothing behind it  
*Reversible:* Behaviour yes, geometry no. Restore light and NEW leaves emerge with correct short petioles and flat carriage, and bloom resumes in 4-8 weeks - but the already-stretched leaves keep their length and angle forever, so a recovering plant reads as a flat young rosette sitting inside a splayed ring of long-petioled old leaves. Growers strip the stretched tier and rebury the neck. The neck length gained is permanent unless the plant is repotted deeper.

### Low humidity / dry air (below ~35% RH), typically winter heating or a forced-air vent

Attacks the flowers first and almost exclusively - bud blast. Buds stop enlarging, shrivel from the apex, turn parchment-tan, and drop off the pedicel, leaving a peduncle carrying nothing but 2-4 mm bare green stubs. Open flowers brown at the lobe margins in a 1 mm papery rim, lose substance so the petals go limp and slightly translucent, and last 3-5 days instead of 2-3 weeks. On foliage, the outermost leaves develop a thin (1-2 mm) tan crisp band on the margin and the leaf TIP curls under, while the blade body stays green and turgid - the tell is that the leaf is dry only at its outline. Soil dries faster so drought symptoms often stack on top.

*Onset:* Bud blast at 5-10 days of dry air; leaf margin crisping 2-3 weeks  
*Reversible:* Yes for future flowering - raise humidity and the next flush of buds develops normally within 2-3 weeks. Blasted buds are gone; that cyme does not regenerate. Browned leaf margins are dead tissue and stay.

### Cold air / chilling injury - draft, cold windowpane, an unheated room, an outdoor trip

Directional and fast. Leaves on the side facing the cold source - typically the ones touching cold window glass - go first: the blade darkens, loses its velvet matte and takes on a glassy, water-soaked translucency, then goes uniformly limp, drooping as a whole leaf without the graceful catenary of drought. Over the following 2-4 days that tissue dries and pales to grey-tan, often as an irregular blotch bounded by a vein rather than following the margin. Petals turn translucent and collapse into slumped rags within hours. The crown stops producing: no new leaf unfurls and no peduncle appears for weeks afterwards, so the plant renders frozen in time. At 13-16 C without frost, symptoms are subtler - just a dulled, slightly greyed rosette with a hard stop to all growth and bloom.

*Onset:* Below 7 C: hours to 2 days. 7-13 C: 2-5 days. 13-16 C: no acute injury, but growth and bloom halt within about a week  
*Reversible:* Damaged tissue never recovers - collapsed leaves must be removed. The plant itself usually survives a brief chill and resumes growth once returned to 18-24 C, but there is a 4-8 week bloom blackout while the crown restarts, which is far longer than the player will expect. A hard freeze through the crown is fatal within 24 h.

### Fertiliser salt accumulation / over-feeding / softened water; the rim-contact injury needs only normal feeding plus an unwaxed clay rim

A hard geometric line, which makes it unmistakable. A white-to-tan crystalline crust builds on the outer pot rim and as a pale salt bloom on the soil surface. Every petiole that rests across that rim develops a brown, dry, corky, constricted band 3-8 mm wide EXACTLY at the contact line; the leaf distal to the band loses turgor and hangs straight down while its own blade is still green and undamaged, and the rest of the plant looks entirely healthy. Independently, over-feeding burns leaf margins tan from the tip back, stunts and tightens the centre growth into small hard brittle leaves, and either suppresses flowers or produces undersized, short-lived ones. Root damage from salt eventually mimics drought droop in wet soil.

*Onset:* Rim burn 4-10 weeks of accumulation, then the affected petiole collapses over 2-4 days. Generalised salt burn and centre stunting 6-12 weeks  
*Reversible:* The plant is easily saved - leach with 4x pot volume of tepid water, scrape the rim, wax it or fit a foil collar. Individual burned petioles are severed functionally and those leaves are lost permanently. Centre stunting grows out over 4-8 weeks; margin burn is permanent on those leaves.

### Cyclamen mite (Phytonemus pallidus) infestation, usually introduced on a new plant

The rosette becomes normal at the edge and monstrous in the middle. The crown centre stops expanding and forms a tight grey-green knot of small, hard, brittle leaves, cupped sharply UPWARD, with visibly denser and coarser hair so the knot reads greyish and fuzzy against the normal green outer tiers. New leaves emerge already stunted, twisted, and distorted, often with the two halves of the blade unequal. Buds fail to open or open lopsided and streaked. The outer two tiers stay fully normal for weeks, so the silhouette is a healthy flat plate with a clenched grey fist at its centre - the pest itself is microscopic and never visible. (Thrips is a related but distinct look: yellow pollen scattered across the lower petals, emptied anthers, and pale silvery stippling on the petals.)

*Onset:* 2-4 weeks from infestation to visible crown distortion; spreads to neighbouring plants within days  
*Reversible:* Poorly. Miticide treatment or a 20-minute 43 C water bath can save a valued plant but the distorted growth never straightens and 6-10 weeks of clean new growth are needed to look normal. Standard grower practice is to discard the plant and quarantine everything within 30 cm. Model this as contagious between specimens in the player's collection.

### Powdery mildew - high humidity combined with stagnant air, crowding, and cool nights

Discrete circular colonies of flat, dry, white-grey powder 3-10 mm across appear on the UPPER leaf surface, on peduncles, and on petals, then coalesce into a continuous dusty film. Beneath a colony the leaf tissue yellows and later goes brown and thin. Flowers get a dulling grey-white bloom over the corolla, distort, and abort. The powder sits on top of the velvet, muting its sheen without changing the leaf's shape at first; heavily affected leaves later curl and dry.

*Onset:* Colonies visible 5-10 days after infection; coalescence and flower loss by 2-3 weeks  
*Reversible:* Yes - improve airflow, reduce crowding, remove affected flowers and leaves, treat. New growth is clean within 1-2 weeks. Leaves that yellowed under a colony stay marked.

### Suckering - untended axillary crowns, favoured by high light, generous feeding, and lack of grooming

Not disease, but it wrecks the silhouette and the bloom. A small secondary rosette 1-3 cm across appears wedged in a leaf axil, forcing the surrounding petioles out of the flat plane and tilting the main rosette so the plant is visibly asymmetric from above. As it grows, both crowns compete: flower count falls by roughly half and peduncles emerge shorter and fewer. A three- or four-crowned plant looks bushy and full while flowering barely at all - a trap for the player, who reads 'more leaves' as 'healthier'.

*Onset:* Sucker visible at 2-3 weeks, symmetry loss obvious at 5-8 weeks, bloom decline at 6-10 weeks  
*Reversible:* Yes, fully, if the sucker is pinched out early with a pencil tip or a knife blade; it leaves a small 2-4 mm scar on the crown and the rosette re-flattens over a few weeks. Left for months, removal means cutting away established tissue and the rosette stays permanently one-sided.

## Lifecycle

1. REST / VEGETATIVE (game day 0-1): no peduncles. The crown unfurls one new leaf pair; leaves emerge tiny, pale, near-vertical and tightly furled at the centre, and rotate down to horizontal as they expand. Rosette diameter creeps up ~1-2%. This is the plant's baseline state and the state the player sees after any deadheading.
2. BUD INITIATION (day 1-2): pea-sized green bud clusters appear in leaf axils of tier 2, sitting BELOW the leaf canopy, half-hidden. Peduncle 1-2 cm. Two to four axils usually initiate together, staggered by a few hours. This stage is the reward signal for a week of correct care and should be legible at a glance.
3. PEDUNCLE ELONGATION & COLOUR BREAK (day 2-3): peduncles extend to 5-10 cm, threading up between petioles, and the cyme rises to or just above canopy level. Buds swell to 6-10 mm and the corolla colour shows through the calyx as a violet slit before opening. Most fragile stage: bud blast from dry air, heat, or drought happens here.
4. ANTHESIS / PEAK BLOOM (day 3-5): flowers open sequentially, one or two a day, so a cyme carries open flowers, spent flowers, and unopened buds simultaneously - never model a cyme as all-open-at-once. Peak is 4-8 open corollas per plant across 2-4 cymes, held 1-3 cm above the leaves, anthers bright yellow. This is the visual payoff state.
5. SENESCENCE (day 5-6): the oldest corollas lose substance, go translucent and slightly greyer, the lobes fold back and inward, then the whole corolla abscises cleanly at the top of the calyx, leaving a green calyx cup and a bare pedicel. Indoors, no pollination occurs, so no capsule forms - the calyx simply dries and drops. Petals litter the leaf surface and, left there, invite Botrytis.
6. GROOMING / TURNOVER (day 6-7): the spent peduncle yellows and is removed (or dries and falls). The oldest outer leaf tier yellows from the margin inward and abscises, adding 0.2-0.5 mm to the neck and a scar to the crown. Fresh axils on the newest tier initiate the next flush, and the cycle restarts. A plant groomed at this point flowers more heavily next flush than one left alone - grooming should be a mechanically real action, not cosmetic.

**Onto a one-week season.** HONEST ANSWER FIRST: this species has no seasons. Streptocarpus ionanthus is an equatorial montane understorey perennial with no dormancy, no photoperiod trigger, and no annual cycle. Well grown, it flowers 8-10 months a year in overlapping flushes. There is no winter form to model. Reduced winter bloom in cultivation is entirely a light-and-temperature artefact, not an endogenous rhythm. So a 'season' here must map onto the BLOOM FLUSH, which is the only real cycle the plant has.

THE MAPPING: one real bloom flush runs about 5-7 weeks - axillary bud initiation to peduncle emergence ~10-14 days, emergence to first open flower ~14-21 days, each individual corolla lasts 2-3 weeks, a full cyme finishes over 4-6 weeks. Compressing that into 7 days is roughly a 6x speedup. Use 6x for everything flower-related and the rhythm reads true.

WHAT THE COMPRESSION DISTORTS, stated plainly:

(1) FOLIAGE AND FLOWERS RUN ON DIFFERENT CLOCKS AND CANNOT SHARE ONE. A new leaf pair appears at the crown every 7-10 real days; a leaf takes 4-6 weeks to reach full size; a rosette takes 6-12 months to mature from a plantlet and 8-12 months from a leaf cutting to first bloom. At 6x, a week of game time buys about six weeks of foliage growth - roughly one new leaf tier and maybe 8% more rosette diameter. That is nearly invisible. Options, in order of honesty: (a) accept it - the rosette barely changes in a week and the flowers carry the visual narrative, which is exactly what a real violet does; (b) run foliage on a separate, faster clock and be explicit in the design doc that the two are decoupled; (c) do NOT scale foliage growth to match bloom, because a violet that visibly grows a whole new rosette in a week is a different, fictional plant.

(2) DAMAGE MUST NOT BE COMPRESSED. Decline in this species is already fast in real time - wilt in 2-4 days, ring spot in 48 hours, crown rot to death in 1-3 weeks. Recovery is slow: damaged leaves never repair, and cosmetic recovery requires growing out 2-3 new tiers, which is 2-4 real months. If you run damage at 6x too, the plant dies in about a day and a half of play and the punishment stops teaching anything. RECOMMENDATION: run bloom at 6x and damage at approximately 1x - one real day of neglect equals one game day of neglect. The player then gets a plant that flowers on a satisfying weekly rhythm but punishes on the true biological schedule. The asymmetry between fast harm and slow healing is the truest thing about this species and should be preserved rather than smoothed.

(3) INDIVIDUAL FLOWER LIFESPAN LOSES ITS MEANING. A real corolla lasts 2-3 weeks, which at 6x is about 2.5 game days - fine. But the fact that a well-grown violet can be continuously in bloom for months, with flushes overlapping so the plant is never bare, disappears if each week is treated as a discrete season with a bare start. Let flushes OVERLAP: a healthy plant should enter game day 0 already carrying a late-stage cyme from the previous week. A plant that goes completely flowerless between weeks is being read by the player as failure, and for this species it genuinely is.

(4) THE NECK IS THE ONLY HONEST LONG-TERM CLOCK. Leaf turnover, neck elongation, and accumulated permanent scars (ring spots, burn margins, snapped-leaf stubs) accrue over months and are what actually distinguishes a well-kept two-year-old plant from a neglected one. Keep these on real-time-ish accumulation and they become the game's true record of care, doing the work that a growth curve cannot do in a week.

**The hard part.** 1. THE VELVET IS THE WHOLE IDENTITY, AND IT IS NOT A TEXTURE. Every blade and petiole is covered in 0.3-1.5 mm translucent white multicellular trichomes at 20-60 per mm2, standing 20-60 degrees off the surface. Rendered as a plain diffuse green leaf the plant instantly reads as plastic - this is the single most common failure. You need three layers: a velvet/sheen BSDF term that brightens sharply at grazing angles (the pale halo around every leaf edge is retro-reflection off hair, not a rim light), a normal-map or shell-texture fuzz for mid-distance, and ACTUAL hair curves on the silhouette and on the upper surface within ~30 cm of camera. Hair colour is near-white and translucent, so it desaturates the green wherever the surface turns away from the viewer - the leaf appears lighter at its edges than at its centre, the opposite of a smooth leaf.

2. THE HAIRS CHANGE HOW WATER BEHAVES, AND WATER IS A GAME MECHANIC. Trichomes make the surface superhydrophobic: droplets sit as near-spherical beads 2-5 mm across, resting on the hair tips with an air gap beneath, and they persist for hours. If you render water on this leaf as a wetting film or a flattened meniscus, it is wrong, and it is wrong in exactly the moment the player is watering - the highest-attention moment in the game. The bead should also refract the green beneath and cast a bright caustic. This matters doubly because standing droplets are the mechanism behind ring spot and Botrytis.

3. WILT MUST BEND THE PETIOLE, NOT THE BLADE. The petiole is a fleshy hydrostat about 4-6 mm thick; when turgor drops it sags progressively along its length into a shallow catenary, while the blade itself stays comparatively flat and simply rotates with the petiole tip, adding only a modest downward roll at its own margins. Applying a single bend deformer to the whole leaf produces limp cooked spinach - a completely different plant. Rig it as a 4-6 joint petiole chain with stiffness falling off toward the tip, driven by a per-leaf turgor scalar, plus a small independent margin-curl blendshape on the blade.

4. WILT PROPAGATES OUTSIDE-IN AND THE ORDER IS DIAGNOSTIC. Outer/oldest tier droops first, crown stays upright; that gradient is how a real grower reads drought at a glance and how your player should. Drive it with a per-leaf age index, not a uniform global droop. Crucially, root rot produces almost the SAME geometry with WET soil - the discrimination has to come from the substrate, the pot weight, the petiole-base discoloration, and gnats, not from leaf pose. Build the soil surface as a real signalling surface (dry/pale/shrunk-from-the-wall vs dark/glossy/algal) because it carries half the diagnostic load.

5. ROSETTE PACKING AND SELF-INTERSECTION. Twenty to forty leaves on a 1.5-2.5 cm crown will interpenetrate under any naive spiral placement. The correct structure is layered: outer tiers sit LOWER and their blades pass UNDER the inner tiers, overlapping like roof shingles, with the whole assembly forming a nearly flat plate. Solve with per-tier vertical offset plus a light collision relax pass; leaves resting on the pot rim should actually contact and deform against it (and that contact line is where salt burn appears, so it must be geometrically real).

6. THE CROWN JUNCTION IS FIDDLY AND ALWAYS LOOKS WRONG FIRST. Twenty-plus thick petioles converging into a 1-2 cm succulent stem, plus a bare scarred neck below with elliptical leaf scars at ~137.5-degree intervals. Most of it is hidden in a healthy plant but it is fully exposed in every interesting state: an etiolated plant, an old plant, a rotting plant, a plant with a sucker. Model it properly rather than hiding it behind foliage.

7. PEDUNCLES COME FROM LEAF AXILS, NOT THE CENTRE. Flower stalks must originate at the base of a specific petiole in tier 2 and thread UP between the surrounding petioles, emerging offset from centre. Spawning the cymes from the crown apex gives a bouquet-in-a-plant-pot look that is instantly, if inarticulably, wrong to anyone who has owned one. Route them with a simple path-of-least-resistance solve against the leaf collision proxies.

8. A CYME IS NEVER ALL-OPEN. Each cyme must simultaneously carry open flowers, spent/abscising ones, and closed buds at multiple sizes, with the sequence advancing over days. Uniformly-open flower clusters are the second-most obvious tell of a synthetic plant.

9. THE ANTHERS ARE A SPECIFIC SHAPE, NOT A BLOB. Two fertile stamens, filaments short and curved, anthers 2.5-4 mm, bright saturated yellow (~#F2C230), held face-to-face as a forward-pointing beak at the throat, with apical pores (poricidal - this is a buzz-pollinated flower). Three staminodes are vestigial and hidden. The style curves up and aside, so the stigma is offset from the anther beak, not in line with it. That yellow beak is the read-at-a-glance identity of the flower.

10. PETAL TRANSLUCENCY. Corolla lobes are 0.2-0.4 mm thick with strong forward scattering - backlit violet lobes glow and show the vein tracery. Without subsurface/thin-film transmission the flowers read as painted card. Deep purple cultivars also have a faint velvety micro-papillate surface that kills specular highlights; the corolla should NOT be glossy.

11. DAMAGE MUST BE PER-LEAF, PERSISTENT, AND ADDRESSED IN UV SPACE - NEVER A GLOBAL COLOUR LERP. A ring spot on leaf 7 belongs to leaf 7 forever, at those coordinates, until that leaf is removed. Give every leaf its own damage mask (R = chlorotic ring spot, G = necrotic/burn, B = bleach, A = rot progress) that is written into at the moment of injury, at the location of injury, and never cleared. A global 'health tint' that slides the whole plant yellow destroys the entire diagnostic premise of the game - the whole point of this species is that damage is local, shaped, and legible.

12. LEAVES SNAP, THEY DO NOT BEND. Physical contact should break a petiole cleanly and permanently, leaving a stub and a crown scar. This is real and it should hurt.

13. SCALE DISCIPLINE. This is a small plant - a mature standard is 25-30 cm across and 10 cm tall, in a 9 cm pot. Rendered without a size cue it photographs identically to something the size of a cabbage. Keep a familiar object in frame, or the trichome and crenation detail will silently mislead the player about scale.

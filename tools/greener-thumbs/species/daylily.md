# Daylily

## Morphology

**Habit.** Herbaceous clump-forming perennial in Asphodelaceae (subfamily Hemerocallidoideae) — NOT a lily, no bulb, no true stem above ground. The structural unit is the FAN: a single short-stemmed shoot whose leaves are strictly distichous (two-ranked, alternating 180 degrees), so every fan is a flattened plane like a hand of cards, not a radial rosette. Leaves emerge from a compressed crown at soil level (a 2-5 cm whitish zone between green leaf bases and roots) and arch outward within that plane. Below the crown: a short thick rhizome plus fleshy spindle-shaped storage roots, white to tan, that fatten in autumn — the drought-survival organ and the reason the plant is nearly unkillable. A clump increases by producing offset fans at the crown edge, each offset rotated roughly 20-60 degrees from its parent, so a mature clump is an interlocking pinwheel of flat planes, not a sphere of leaves. Flowering is on a SCAPE: a leafless, solid, roughly terete stalk rising from the centre of a mature fan, usually taller than the foliage, branched near the top, carrying many buds at staggered maturities. Foliage habit is a cultivar trait with three registered classes — dormant (dies completely to the crown after frost, requires winter chill), semi-evergreen (partial dieback), evergreen (retains foliage, damaged below about -7 C).

**Leaf.** Linear, sessile, sheathing at the base, entire (smooth) margin, glabrous, parallel-veined with no visible reticulation, tapering to a long acuminate point. Cross-section is a shallow-to-deep V — a pronounced keel along the abaxial midrib, deepest near the base (interior angle roughly 100-130 degrees) and flattening toward the tip (150-175 degrees). The keel is the single most diagnostic silhouette feature and is also the turgor gauge: it closes tighter as the leaf dries. Curvature is a cantilever, not a circular arc — the basal 30-55 percent is nearly straight and stiff, then curvature increases monotonically toward the tip, which may hang past vertical on long cultivars. Most leaves carry a slight axial twist (0-90 degrees base to tip), so the blade face rotates as it arches. Colour medium to yellow-green (hex family #4E7A33 to #7FA65A), semi-glossy adaxially, duller and paler abaxially where the keel catches light as a bright line. New spring growth emerges as tightly rolled bright yellow-green spears that unfurl over several days. Older outer leaves of a fan are longer, more strongly arched and darker; the innermost are shorter, erect and paler.

**Flower.** Six tepals in two whorls of three, fused at the base into a perianth tube (floral tube) 1.5-5 cm long — this tube is where the nectar sits and it must be modelled; a six-petal fan with no tube reads as a lily and is wrong. OUTER whorl = 3 sepals, narrower, usually smoother-edged, at 0/120/240 degrees. INNER whorl = 3 petals, distinctly wider (petal width roughly 1.3-2.2x sepal width) and carrying most of the ruffling, at 60/180/300 degrees. Making all six tepals identical is the classic modelling bug. Form ranges from flat-faced through trumpet/funnelform to strongly recurved, to spider forms with narrow strap tepals. Colour covers everything except true blue and pure white: yellows, golds, oranges, melon, pink, red, purple, near-black, cream. Wild species are yellow or tawny orange only (H. fulva tawny orange; H. lilioasphodelus lemon and fragrant); all pinks, reds and purples are hybrid. Standard patterning, concentric outward from centre: a THROAT (green, yellow or gold, 12-35 percent of flower radius, transition slightly diffuse rather than a hard ring), often an EYE or band zone above it in a contrasting darker colour, then the base tepal colour, and frequently a picotee or gold-wire EDGE band 1-6 mm wide. Most cultivars show a paler MIDRIB stripe running the length of each tepal. Six stamens with filaments curving upward in a shallow arc and versatile (centrally hinged, freely swinging) anthers bearing yellow-gold to brown pollen; one style exceeding the anthers by 0.5-2 cm, ending in a small capitate stigma. Tepals are thin and translucent — they glow when backlit. Many cultivars show 'diamond dusting': raised epidermal cells that scatter light as a crystalline sparkle in direct sun. Flowers sit on short pedicels off the scape branches, generally at or above foliage height. CRITICAL: each individual flower lasts about one day. Diurnal cultivars break colour in the bud the evening before, split at the bud tip before dawn, are fully open by mid-morning, begin curling at the tepal edges in afternoon heat, and are collapsed by dusk. Nocturnal cultivars (H. citrina lineage, many fragrant yellows) open late afternoon and stay open until midday the following day. 'Extended' is a registered class meaning open 16+ hours. A spent flower does NOT abscise cleanly: it collapses into a wet, translucent, spirally twisted wad that shrinks to 20-40 percent of its open length, hangs on the pedicel darkening from saturated to brown, and dries to a papery twist over 2-4 days. If not deadheaded, a fertile cultivar may instead set a green three-ridged capsule at that pedicel tip. Fruit is a loculicidal 3-valved capsule with angular glossy black seeds.

## Care

**Light.** Full sun, 6+ hours direct, is optimum for bud count and scape strength. Tolerates 4 hours or bright dappled shade with a large drop in flowering. Cultivar-dependent inversion that a naive model gets backwards: dark cultivars (deep red, purple, near-black) absorb enough radiant heat that their tepals scald in intense afternoon sun, so they want morning sun plus afternoon shade for bloom QUALITY even though that reduces bloom COUNT. Pale yellows, creams and pinks bleach and lose edge saturation in the same conditions. Light therefore needs two separate outputs in the sim: vigour/bud count (monotonic with light) and bloom appearance (cultivar-dependent optimum).

**Water.** About 2.5 cm (1 inch) per week, delivered deep and infrequent rather than shallow and daily; established clumps survive long droughts on their fleshy storage roots. The requirement is NOT flat across the year — the critical window is scape elongation and bud development, when drought causes bud blast (buds abort and drop) even though the foliage still looks fine. Outside that window the plant tolerates neglect almost indefinitely. Overwatering is only dangerous in combination with poor drainage and warmth; standing water at the crown in warm weather is the one reliable killer. Water at the base, not overhead — wet foliage drives leaf streak.

**Temperature.** Growth optimum roughly 18-29 C. Hardy USDA zones 3-9 (dormant cultivars survive soil at -40 C under mulch; evergreen cultivars are damaged below about -7 C and are unreliable below zone 6). Above about 35 C, flower life shortens and dark cultivars melt. Dormant cultivars REQUIRE a winter chill period; grown frost-free they weaken over a few years. Evergreen cultivars in cold zones flush new growth during winter warm spells and lose it to the next freeze. Freeze-thaw cycles heave shallow crowns out of the soil; mulch prevents it.

**Humidity.** Ambient outdoor humidity; the plant itself is not humidity-sensitive. Humidity matters only through disease — warm plus humid plus still air drives daylily rust, leaf streak and bacterial soft rot. Air circulation across the clump is the mitigating variable, not humidity level per se.

**Soil.** Highly adaptable, including clay. Prefers well-drained loam with organic matter, pH 6.0-6.5 (tolerates about 5.5-7.5). Drainage matters far more than fertility. Fertiliser should be light and balanced — excess nitrogen produces lush dark floppy foliage and few scapes.

### What a real grower knows

Gotchas a real grower knows and a naive implementation will get wrong. (1) EVERY FLOWER DIES EVERY DAY AND THAT IS HEALTH. Hemerocallis means 'beauty for a day'. If the health system reads wilted blooms as neglect, the plant is permanently, wrongly sick. Daily bloom senescence must be a scheduled lifecycle event, not a damage state. (2) Spent blooms do not fall off. They hang as wet twisted wads. A plant carrying five days of spent blooms looks terrible and is perfectly healthy — deadheading is a cosmetic and energy chore, not a rescue. (3) Skipping deadheading on a fertile cultivar produces seed capsules that visibly divert energy: fewer or no rebloom scapes later in the season. (4) Underwatering punishes BUDS, not leaves. Drought during scape development blasts buds while foliage still looks acceptable. Modelling drought as leaf-wilt-first is backwards for this species. (5) The plant can lose 100 percent of its foliage and live; the crown and storage roots regenerate a new fan in 10-20 days. To actually kill a daylily you need warm waterlogged soil and crown rot — make that the only death path or the 'forgiving' design premise is a lie. (6) Foliage habit class (dormant / semi-evergreen / evergreen) changes the entire annual silhouette and the cold response; pick one per specimen and state it in the codex. (7) Nocturnal cultivars invert the daily loop entirely — bloom opens late afternoon, still open next morning. If the game's core loop is a morning check-in, a nocturnal specimen is a genuinely different game. (8) Rebloom is cultivar-specific. Most classic cultivars bloom once for 3-5 weeks and are done for the year; only rebloomers ('Stella de Oro', 'Happy Returns') go again. Do not make rebloom universal. (9) 'Spring sickness': emerging spring foliage in many cultivars comes up ragged, twisted, brown-streaked and holed for no reason the player caused, and the plant grows out of it. Realistic, and it must not be scored against the player. (10) Proliferations: small rooted plantlets sometimes form at nodes on the scape and can be snapped off and grown on — a free propagation mechanic that is entirely botanically real. (11) Division every 3-5 years. An undivided clump crowds, the centre thins, fans narrow, flowers shrink. (12) Deer eat daylily buds and scapes preferentially; this is the standard outdoor threat, not aphids. (13) Toxicity is asymmetric and worth getting right: daylilies cause acute fatal kidney failure in CATS, while the buds and flowers are a traditional human food (dried buds sold as golden needles). Do not label it simply 'toxic' or simply 'edible'. (14) It is not a lily and has no bulb. Anything in the art or copy showing a bulb is a bug. (15) A flower opens once and cannot re-open; if a player 'saves' a wilting afternoon flower with water, nothing should happen to that flower. Tomorrow's bud is the reward.

## Decline

### Underwatering during scape elongation and bud development (the moisture-critical window)

Bud blast. Individual buds on the scape stop swelling, lose their green gloss, go pale yellow-tan and slightly translucent at the base, soften, then shrivel and drop from the pedicel — leaving a bare stub 0.5-1.5 cm long standing on the scape branch. Blast runs from the youngest, uppermost buds downward, so a scape ends up with open flowers below and a comb of bare stubs above. Foliage at this stage still looks broadly acceptable, which is the point: the damage is in the flower stalk, not the leaves.

*Onset:* 3-7 real days of dry soil; the stubs remain visible for the rest of that scape's life, roughly 2-3 weeks  
*Reversible:* The plant recovers fully once watered, but the blasted buds are gone permanently — those flowers never happen. Leaves a visible permanent scar on that scape for the season.

### Sustained drought over weeks, outside the bud window

Turgor loss progressing outer-to-inner. Outer (oldest, longest) leaves go first: the keel angle closes from roughly 130 degrees toward 100, so the V deepens and the blade narrows in silhouette; the arch-onset point migrates toward the base so the whole leaf sags, and the tip drops from about 30 degrees below horizontal to 90-110, hanging near the soil. Colour desaturates from yellow-green toward grey-green with a matte loss of specular. Necrosis then advances from the tip backward as a straw-tan papery zone with a 0.5-4 cm yellow transition band between dead and living tissue. The crown and innermost leaves stay upright and green far longer than the outer ones. Under extreme drought the plant deliberately goes dormant: all foliage browns and collapses into a flattened mat over the crown.

*Onset:* 10-21 real days for visible sag; 21-40 for full collapse  
*Reversible:* Almost always fully reversible — this is the species' signature. Green spears push from the crown 10-20 days after water returns and a new fan is established in 3-5 weeks. Already-necrotic leaf tips never re-green; those leaves stay marked until shed or cut. No permanent mark on the crown.

### Overwatering with poor drainage in warm weather — bacterial crown/soft rot (Pectobacterium/Erwinia), or southern blight (Sclerotium rolfsii)

THE KILL PATH, and its signature is the exact inverse of drought. The INNERMOST leaves yellow first — the centre of the fan goes pale, then water-soaked tan, while the outer leaves are still green. The base of the fan at the crown turns soft, wet-looking, translucent tan-brown; the fan loses rigidity as a unit and leans, then topples sideways as a single flat plane rather than leaf by leaf. Pull-test: the whole fan lifts free of the crown with almost no resistance, base mushy and foul-smelling. With southern blight, add white cottony mycelium at the soil line and tan sclerotia the size and colour of mustard seeds. Roots go from firm white-tan to brown and hollow.

*Onset:* 5-14 real days in warm wet soil; an individual fan collapses within 2-3 days of its centre yellowing  
*Reversible:* NOT reversible for the affected fan. Adjacent fans can be saved by cutting out the rotted fan and improving drainage. If the rot reaches the whole crown the plant dies — this should be the game's only true death state for this species.

### Insufficient light (deep or prolonged shade)

Etiolation with no discolouration — the trap is that the plant looks lush. Leaves grow LONGER (up to 1.3x) and NARROWER, the keel opens flatter, the blade goes limp and floppy rather than arching cleanly, and the whole fan leans toward the brightest direction so the fan plane tilts off vertical. Colour stays green or even deepens. The real signal is reproductive: scape count per clump drops sharply, surviving scapes grow taller and thinner and bow or flop under bud weight, branch count falls (3-4 branches down to 0-1), buds per scape collapse (25 down to 5-8), and the flowers that do open are smaller in diameter and paler in base colour.

*Onset:* 14-30 real days for the leaf change; a full season for the flowering collapse to be unmistakable  
*Reversible:* Fully reversible. Restored light produces normal new fans in 3-6 weeks and normal flowering the following season. Stretched leaves stay stretched until shed — no permanent damage.

### Intense direct sun plus heat above about 32 C on a dark-pigmented cultivar — 'melting'

A bloom-quality failure with zero foliage involvement. On dark red, purple or near-black tepals, translucent water-soaked blotches appear on the sun-facing surfaces by late morning, spreading into wet-looking glossy patches with a darker saturated rim. Tepal edges curl inward and downward, ruffles lose definition and go limp, and the affected areas collapse into a wet, blotchy, tacky-looking surface hours before normal dusk senescence. On pale yellow, cream and pink cultivars the equivalent damage is bleaching: the base colour washes toward off-white, the picotee edge and eye zone lose contrast, and the flower reads flat and chalky.

*Onset:* Same day — visible within 3-6 hours of peak sun, on that individual flower only  
*Reversible:* Not reversible for the affected flower, but it was going to die by dusk anyway. Zero lasting harm to the plant; tomorrow's flower opens clean if conditions change. This is the case where 'more sun' is the wrong player action despite improving plant vigour.

### Daylily rust (Puccinia hemerocallidis), driven by warm humid still air

Raised pustules 0.5-1.5 mm, bright orange to yellow-orange, concentrated on the LEAF UNDERSIDE and arranged in broken lines following the parallel veins, densest along the keel. Each pustule sits in a pale yellow halo; on the upper surface the same spots show as faint yellow flecks. The spores are powdery and smear orange when wiped — a strong visual tell and a good interaction cue. Heavy infection turns whole leaves yellow, then dries them from the tip back, and pustules go darker and crustier late in the season.

*Onset:* 7-14 real days from infection to visible pustules; leaf yellowing 7-14 days after that  
*Reversible:* Reversible at plant level: cutting foliage to about 10 cm removes the infection and regrowth comes back clean in 3-5 weeks. Infected leaves themselves never clear. Recurs from spores if humidity and crowding persist.

### Leaf streak (Aureobasidium microstictum), from overhead watering and persistently wet foliage

A yellow stripe running ALONG THE MIDRIB, starting mid-leaf and extending toward the tip, studded with reddish-brown lens-shaped spots 2-8 mm arranged in a line on that midrib. The leaf then dies from the tip backward, the dead zone bounded by the same yellow stripe. Distinguish from drought necrosis by geometry: drought browns the whole tip evenly and symmetrically across both halves of the blade; leaf streak is a centreline defect with discrete spots on it, often asymmetric between the two halves.

*Onset:* 10-20 real days after a wet-foliage period  
*Reversible:* Reversible by removing affected leaves and switching to base watering; new leaves emerge clean. Affected leaves are permanently marked.

### Hard freeze on an evergreen or semi-evergreen cultivar, or a late frost on new spring growth

Leaves go dark and water-soaked, then translucent, then limp within hours. Turgor is lost completely and simultaneously across the whole leaf — unlike drought, which progresses tip-to-base — so the fan drops flat rather than arching down. Within a day or two the collapsed tissue turns tan and slimy, then dries to grey-brown papery ribbons. In dormant cultivars this same event is the NORMAL autumn transition and must not be scored as damage.

*Onset:* Hours to 2 real days  
*Reversible:* Foliage is a total loss; the crown survives within the cultivar's hardiness range and regrows in spring. If the crown itself freezes (an evergreen cultivar well below its zone) the plant dies.

### Deer or rabbit browsing

Scapes sheared off at 15-40 cm leaving a blunt, ragged, fibrous-torn end — deer tear rather than cut, so the stub is frayed, not clean — with buds stripped from any remaining branches. Leaves cropped to a fairly uniform 10-25 cm across the whole clump with torn, whitened tips. The silhouette becomes a flat-topped tuft with cut stubs standing above it.

*Onset:* Overnight — a single event, not a progression  
*Reversible:* Foliage regrows in 3-5 weeks. The season's flowers on browsed scapes are permanently lost. No harm to the crown.

### Overcrowding — clump not divided for 5+ years

Fan count climbs but each fan shrinks: leaves narrow by 30-50 percent and shorten, fans crowd shoulder to shoulder into a dense flat-topped hedge, and the centre of the clump thins or goes bare so the mass reads as a ring rather than a mound. Scapes are shorter and thinner, branch count drops, and flower diameter falls 20-40 percent below the cultivar's registered size. The whole clump looks busy and unwell without any single leaf looking damaged.

*Onset:* Season-scale — 2-4 real years, showing as declining flower size year over year  
*Reversible:* Fully reversible by lifting and dividing; divisions return to full size in one to two seasons. Nothing is permanently lost.

### Excess nitrogen fertiliser

Foliage becomes noticeably darker green, wider, longer and softer, arching more loosely and flopping outward so the fan plane loses definition; leaves take on a slightly glossy, almost swollen look. Meanwhile scape count drops toward zero and any scapes that do form are soft and lean. A big, dark, healthy-looking, flowerless plant — which is exactly the trap.

*Onset:* 14-30 real days for the foliage change; the flowering deficit shows over the following bloom cycle  
*Reversible:* Reversible — stop feeding and normal flowering resumes the next cycle. Soft foliage is meanwhile more attractive to aphids and more rot-prone.

### Daylily gall midge (Contarinia quinquenotata), or thrips feeding inside the buds

Gall midge: individual buds swell abnormally FAT and SHORT — roughly half normal length at nearly double normal width, lumpy and distorted rather than smoothly spindle-shaped — then fail to open, yellow, and rot in place on the scape while neighbouring buds develop normally. Thrips: the bud develops normally but the flower opens with silvered or brown streaks running lengthwise along the tepals and asymmetric distortion, one or two tepals failing to expand fully so the flower opens visibly lopsided.

*Onset:* Gall midge distortion visible 7-14 days before that bud's scheduled opening; thrips damage visible only on the day the flower opens  
*Reversible:* The affected buds and flowers are lost permanently. The plant is otherwise unharmed and later buds can be clean. Removing and destroying deformed buds reduces the next generation.

## Lifecycle

1. WINTER DORMANCY (dormant cultivars, roughly Dec-Feb): no foliage at all. Visible geometry is a bare crown at soil level with last year's leaf bases as a small tan collar, plus mulch. Semi-evergreen cultivars keep a short ragged 10-20 cm tuft; evergreens keep near-full foliage with frost-marked tips.
2. EMERGENCE (early spring, 2-4 weeks): tightly rolled bright yellow-green spears push vertically from the crown at 2-4 cm per day. Colour markedly lighter and more yellow than mature foliage. Many cultivars show 'spring sickness' here — ragged, holed, brown-streaked, twisted new leaves the plant simply grows past. Not player-caused, must not be scored as damage.
3. FOLIAGE BUILD (mid-late spring, 3-6 weeks): leaves unfurl, lengthen and begin arching; the distichous fan plane becomes clearly readable. Colour deepens to full green. Offset fans appear at the clump edge. Storage roots, depleted by winter, refill.
4. SCAPE EMERGENCE (late spring, 10-20 days): a smooth round leafless stalk pushes from the centre of each mature fan and elongates 2-5 cm per day, overtaking the foliage. It branches near the top and buds appear as tiny green spindles at the branch tips. THIS IS THE MOISTURE-CRITICAL WINDOW — drought here blasts buds.
5. BUD DEVELOPMENT (10-25 days, overlapping bloom): buds elongate from 1 cm to a full 6-10 cm and curve gently upward. A single scape carries buds at every maturity simultaneously — fat colour-broken buds low down and pinhead green buds at the tips. The evening before opening a bud shows 'colour break': the cultivar colour appears at the bud tip.
6. BLOOM (3-5 weeks per cultivar): the scape opens flowers sequentially from the base branch upward. Each individual flower lasts about one day. A mature multi-branched scape opens 1-3 flowers per day; a mature clump with 6-12 scapes carries 4-15 open flowers on a good day. Spent flowers accumulate as twisted wads unless deadheaded.
7. POST-BLOOM / SEED SET (2-4 weeks): scapes yellow from the top down, dry to tan and go brittle; a ripe scape snaps cleanly out of the fan at the base. Undeadheaded fertile cultivars carry swollen green three-ridged capsules that later split to release glossy black seed. Rebloom cultivars only: a second, usually shorter, scape flush.
8. SUMMER PLATEAU (4-8 weeks): pure foliage. Leaves at maximum length and arch; older outer leaves begin browning at the tips regardless of care, which is normal ageing and not neglect. Fan multiplication continues underground.
9. AUTUMN SENESCENCE (3-5 weeks): foliage yellows from the tips inward, green to yellow to tan. Storage roots fatten and firm. After the first hard frost, dormant-cultivar leaves collapse flat as a mat over the crown within a day or two.

**Onto a one-week season.** HONEST ANSWER FIRST: this species does not compress cleanly, and the reason is the same reason you chose it. The daylily has exactly one true real-time element — a flower lasts one day, and 'one day' means one actual solar day. Everything else is annual. If a 7-day season equals a year, one game day equals about 52 real days, which would make each flower live about 30 game minutes. You cannot have both. RECOMMENDATION: run a DUAL CLOCK and say so in the design docs rather than pretending it is one timescale. Anchor the bloom at 1:1 — one flower opens at the player's morning and dies by their evening, so one game day is one flower's life, because that mapping is free and it is the species' entire identity. Compress everything else against it and accept that the compression ratio is not uniform. WHAT THIS DISTORTS, EXPLICITLY: (1) BLOOM DURATION. A real cultivar blooms 3-5 weeks and opens 15-40 flowers per scape. Seven days of bloom shows you maybe 7 flowers if you model one scape. The honest fix is to model a CLUMP, not a single fan: 6-12 fans, 6-12 scapes, 4-15 open flowers simultaneously per day, which is exactly what a real established clump does. That fills the week without lying about any single flower. Do NOT solve it by making flowers last two days. (2) BUD ECONOMY. If a scape must be exhausted by day 7 you will be tempted to model scapes with 6 buds. Real scapes carry 8-40. Keep the real count and let scapes carry unopened buds into the next season, or let the season simply end mid-bloom — a scape still holding buds at season's end is more truthful than one that conveniently runs dry. (3) DORMANCY. A dormant cultivar spends about four months of twelve as a bare crown; honest compression gives that roughly two of seven days with no plant on screen. That is bad gameplay and you will be tempted to delete it. Two defensible options: pick a SEMI-EVERGREEN cultivar as the game specimen so the plant is never fully bare and dormancy compresses to a short ragged tuft — a real registered cultivar class, not a cheat; or keep a dormant cultivar and treat winter as a between-season interstitial rather than in-season days. State which you chose in the codex entry. (4) RECOVERY ASYMMETRY — the most important distortion. Damage here is fast and recovery is slow: crown rot kills a fan in 5-14 real days, but regrowing that fan takes 3-5 real weeks. Under uniform compression both collapse to 'instant', which makes the forgiving plant feel fragile and makes rescue feel weightless. Decouple them deliberately: let damage accrue on compressed time, but make crown-driven regrowth take a large fraction of a season, so losing a fan costs most of a week. That preserves the true emotional shape of the species, which is 'very hard to kill, slow to forgive'. (5) EMERGENCE SPEED. New spears genuinely grow 2-4 cm per day, fast enough to read day over day at 1:1. Compressing that further makes the plant look animated rather than alive. Consider running vegetative growth near real time as well and compressing only the long summer plateau and autumn senescence. (6) SEASON-SCALE FAILURES DO NOT FIT AT ALL. Overcrowding takes 3-5 years to show and division is a 3-5 year decision. These cannot be honestly squeezed into a 7-day loop. Either surface them as multi-season meta-progression across many in-game seasons, or leave them out — do not shrink a four-year process into four days. (7) DISEASE LATENCY. Rust takes 7-14 days from infection to visible pustules. Compressed, cause and symptom land on the same day and the player learns nothing about the delay. If you compress it, keep at least one game day of latency so the lesson 'today's humid crowding is next week's orange leaves' survives.

| `leafLength` | 20-90 cm; outer leaves 1.2-1.5x the inner leaves of the same fan | Overall scale. The inner-to-outer length ratio is what makes a fan look grown rather than assembled. |
| `leafWidth` | 1.0-3.5 cm maximum, occurring 25-35 percent up from the base, tapering to an acuminate point | Blade proportion. A leaf widest at its base looks like an iris, not a daylily. |
| `keelAngle` | 100-175 degrees interior V; deepest at the base, opening toward the tip; closes a further 20-40 degrees at low turgor | Cross-section, the bright abaxial highlight line, and the primary visual read of hydration. |
| `archOnset` | 0.30-0.55 of leaf length (the straight, rigid fraction before curvature begins); migrates toward 0.15 at low turgor | Cantilever character. A circular-arc leaf looks rubbery; straight-then-increasingly-curved is the correct profile. |
| `tipDeclination` | 20-110 degrees below horizontal at the tip | Droop. Combined with archOnset it is the whole posture of the plant. |
| `leafAxialTwist` | 0-90 degrees of rotation from base to tip | Breaks the flat-ribbon look and catches light unevenly along the blade, which is most of what makes foliage read as real. |
| `turgor` | 0.0-1.0 | Simultaneously: keelAngle (closes), archOnset (drops toward the base), tipDeclination (increases), colour saturation (toward grey-green), specular intensity (drops). Must drive all five together or wilting looks like an animation rather than dehydration. |
| `necrosisFraction + transitionBandWidth` | 0.0-1.0 advancing from the tip; band 0.5-4 cm | Tip dieback. The yellow transition band between dead and living tissue is what sells it; a hard green-to-brown boundary reads as a texture error. |
| `declineDirection` | Enum: outer-fan tip-to-base (drought) vs inner-fan base-to-tip (rot) vs whole-leaf simultaneous (freeze) | Which mask the yellowing uses. Three genuinely different geometries — collapsing them into one generic 'yellowing' parameter destroys the diagnostic skill the game teaches. |
| `scapeCount / scapeHeight / branchCount` | 0-3 scapes per mature fan; height 28-120 cm; branches 0-5, ascending, upper shorter than lower | Bloom architecture and how far the flowers stand above the foliage. |
| `budsPerScape + budStageDistribution` | 8-40 buds; every scape must simultaneously hold buds at stages 0-5, weighted so lower branches are more mature | The single most important flowering realism cue. A scape with uniformly identical buds is instantly, obviously wrong. |
| `budStage (per bud, independent)` | 0 pinhead green / 1 elongating spindle / 2 full size with colour break at the tip / 3 open flower / 4 collapsed spent wad / 5 dry twist or green capsule | The daily loop. Stage 2 to 3 is the overnight transition the player wakes up to; stage 3 to 4 is the one they must learn not to fear. |
| `flowerDiameter` | 5-20 cm; miniature 5-8, standard 10-14, large 15-20 | Cultivar identity. Must stay consistent with scapeHeight and ploidy. |
| `sepalWidthRatio` | 0.45-0.75 of petal width | The outer-versus-inner whorl distinction. Setting this to 1.0 (all six tepals identical) is the most common and most conspicuous modelling error for this genus. |
| `tepalRecurve + ruffleAmplitude/Frequency` | Recurve 0-90 degrees; ruffles 0-12 lobes per margin at 0-1.5 cm amplitude, applied mainly to petals | Form class, from flat-faced through trumpet to recurved to spider. Ruffles need real edge-length excess, not a normal map. |
| `throatRadius / eyeZoneWidth / edgeBandWidth` | Throat 0.12-0.35 of flower radius; eye 0-0.35; edge band 0.1-0.6 cm | The concentric colour pattern. Transitions should be narrow 2-5 mm gradients, never hard rings. |
| `perianthTubeLength` | 1.5-5.0 cm | The fused base where all six tepals join and where the flower attaches to the pedicel. Omitting it turns the flower into a lily. |
| `tepalThickness + translucency` | 0.03-0.08 cm (diploids thinner, tetraploids thicker and stiffer) | Subsurface scattering and the backlit glow. An opaque tepal shader destroys the flower. |
| `diamondDusting` | 0.0-1.0 micro-facet density on the adaxial epidermis | The crystalline sparkle in direct sun. Cultivar-specific: absent on many, striking on some. |
| `ploidy` | Diploid or tetraploid — a preset that co-varies several parameters at once | Tetraploids: larger flowers, thicker and more ruffled tepals, stiffer thicker scapes, wider leaves. Diploids: narrower, more graceful, more recurve. Exposing this as one switch stops artists building botanically impossible combinations. |
| `spentWadTwist + shrinkFactor` | 180-540 degrees of axial twist; shrink to 0.20-0.40 of open length | The daily death of every flower. A required deformation, not a colour change. |
| `foliageHabitClass` | dormant / semi-evergreen / evergreen | Winter silhouette (bare crown / short ragged tuft / near-full foliage) and the cold-damage threshold. Must be a per-specimen property, chosen once and stated. |
| `bloomChronotype` | diurnal / extended / nocturnal | When the flower opens and closes relative to the player's session. Nocturnal cultivars open late afternoon and are still open next morning — a different core loop, so lock this per specimen. |

**The hard part.** 1. THE SPENT BLOOM. Every flower dies every day, so the collapsed-wad state is on screen constantly and it is the hardest asset in the plant. It is not a wilted version of the open flower — it is a wet, translucent, spirally twisted tube shrunk to about a fifth of its length, hanging limp off the pedicel and darkening over 2-4 days into a dry papery corkscrew. You cannot reach it by scaling or drooping the open mesh; it needs a dedicated twist-plus-shrink deformer with the tepal tips converging and the perianth tube pinching. Budget real time for this. If it looks like a shrunken flower rather than a wet rag, the plant reads as sick every single day of the game.

2. THE FAN PLANE. Daylily leaves are strictly two-ranked in a single plane. Every instinct in a procedural foliage generator is to distribute leaves radially, and that instinct produces something unmistakably not-a-daylily that is very hard for a non-botanist reviewer to name as wrong. Enforce the 180-degree alternation, allow only a few degrees of splay, and get all your variety from the rotation of WHOLE FANS relative to each other — never from breaking the phyllotaxy inside a fan.

3. LEAF CURVATURE. It is a cantilever under self-weight, not an arc: rigid and near-straight for the basal third to half, then curvature increasing monotonically to a tip that may hang past vertical. Curvature must be a function of both length and turgor, and the inflection point must MOVE toward the base as turgor drops. A fixed-shape leaf that merely rotates downward when dry looks like a hinge, not a wilt.

4. RUFFLES. The ruffled margin of a modern cultivar exists because the petal edge has more arc length than the boundary it attaches to. It must be true geometry — sine or noise displacement along the edge normal with real added length — because it silhouettes against the sky and reads on the outline, where a normal map contributes nothing.

5. TEPAL SHADING. Tepals are 0.3-0.8 mm of translucent tissue. Backlit they glow and the venation shows through; front-lit with diamond dusting they sparkle; dark red and purple cultivars have a velvety nap that eats specular highlights and goes almost black at grazing angles. Three distinct optical behaviours in one surface. A standard opaque diffuse-plus-specular shader fails all three, and the dark cultivars in particular will look like moulded plastic.

6. SIMULTANEOUS BUD STATES ON ONE SCAPE. Real scapes carry pinhead buds, elongating buds, colour-broken buds, an open flower, yesterday's wad and last week's dry twist all at once. The scape therefore cannot be a single asset with a bloom toggle — every pedicel needs independent state and independent geometry, and the state distribution must be spatially biased (mature low, immature high). This is a data-structure decision, so make it before any art is built.

7. THE THROAT GRADIENT. The throat-to-tepal transition is a narrow diffuse band, a few millimetres wide, that follows each tepal's own curvature down into the perianth tube. Painted as a flat ring on a UV it looks stamped on. It also has to survive the tepal deforming through opening and collapse, so make it procedural in the shader rather than baked into a texture.

8. HONEST DECLINE DIRECTIONALITY. Drought damage travels outer-leaf-first, tip-to-base. Rot travels inner-leaf-first, base-to-tip. Freeze hits the whole leaf at once. If all three are implemented as one generic yellowing parameter, the player can never learn to tell them apart — and telling them apart is the entire skill this species exists to teach. Build them as separate, oppositely-directed masks from the start; retrofitting this later means rebuilding the damage shader.

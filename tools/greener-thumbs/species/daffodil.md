# yellow_daffodil

> Generated as a design specification, not a botany reference. Every
> decline mode below is written to be *renderable* — geometry and colour,
> not adjectives.

## Morphology

**Habit.** Acaulescent bulbous geophyte (Amaryllidaceae). There is no true stem above ground and no branching: everything emerges directly from the neck of an underground tunicate bulb. Each mature 'nose' (growth point) produces one basal fan of 4-6 linear leaves plus, if the bulb is large enough and has been sufficiently chilled, one leafless flowering scape. The fan is distichous at its base — the leaves emerge in two ranks and then splay radially — so a single nose reads as a flattened, slightly twisted fan rather than a rosette. Leaves emerge erect and progressively arch outward 15-40 degrees from vertical as they extend. The scape overtops the foliage by 5-15 cm in a healthy plant. Over successive years the bulb splits into offsets at the axils of the fleshy scales, producing a congested multi-nosed clump; each offset has its own fan, its own tilt (up to about 20 degrees off vertical) and its own depth, so a mature clump is emphatically NOT a radially symmetric arrangement about one point. Below ground: an ovoid to pyriform tunicate bulb with a papery mid-brown outer tunic, a flat basal plate at the bottom bearing white unbranched fleshy roots, and concentric fleshy scales enclosing the preformed shoot. Model as N. pseudonarcissus and its large trumpet cultivars (RHS Division 1, colour code 2Y-Y — self-yellow perianth and corona), e.g. 'Dutch Master' or 'King Alfred'; wild N. pseudonarcissus is roughly 60% of these dimensions.

**Leaf.** Linear to narrowly strap-shaped (loraceous), basal, sessile — no petiole. Emerges from the bulb neck sheathed in a membranous cataphyll, so the lowest 3-6 cm is pale whitish-green grading upward into full colour. Apex BLUNT/rounded (obtuse), never pointed — a tapered tip is the wrong genus. Margins entire and smooth. Venation parallel, many fine unbranched veins with no midrib prominence, faintly visible as longitudinal striations and much more visible when backlit. Cross-section is a shallow V or keel, deepest near the base (keel depth about 20% of blade width) and flattening toward the tip; blade thickness 0.5-1.0 mm. The blade twists slowly along its length, roughly 10-25 degrees per 10 cm, with the direction varying leaf to leaf. Colour glaucous blue-green — a dusty, matte, slightly grey-blue green (roughly RGB 122,150,120 base with a pale blue-grey wax layer over it, reading near 150,170,155 at grazing angles); noticeably bluer than most garden foliage. The surface carries a thick epicuticular wax bloom that kills specular reflection and rubs off where handled, leaving darker, glossier green streaks. Water beads and rolls off. Outer leaves are the longest and arch the most; inner leaves are shorter, narrower and more erect.

**Flower.** Solitary per scape (Division 1, trumpet). Held at 60-90 degrees from the vertical — facing outward and slightly downward, NOT upward — with the bend occurring as a sharp node at the inferior ovary rather than as a curve in the stem. Structure from the stem outward: (1) a papery, membranous, tan-buff SPATHE, a single dry bract 3-5 cm long that sheathed the bud and splits down one side, persisting as a translucent hood behind the flower; (2) the green INFERIOR OVARY, ovoid, 1.0-1.5 cm, faintly three-ridged; (3) a slender FLORAL TUBE 1.5-2.5 cm, green at the base grading to yellow; (4) six PERIANTH SEGMENTS (3 outer sepals and 3 inner petals, near-identical, so read as 6 tepals) — ovate to broadly lanceolate, 3.0-4.5 cm long by 1.5-2.5 cm wide, each with a small sharp apiculate point at the tip, overlapping 20-40% at their bases to form a continuous six-pointed star roughly perpendicular to the flower axis, giving a face diameter of 7-10 cm; (5) the CORONA or trumpet, a fused tube arising from the fused base of the tepals (not from a separate receptacle), 3.5-5.0 cm long — as long as or longer than the tepals, which is the defining character of the division — with a mouth 2.5-4.0 cm across, expanded and everted at the rim into a frilled, crenate margin of about six shallow lobes carrying a finer irregular ripple, and faint longitudinal ribbing on the outer wall. Inside the corona: six STAMENS inserted in the tube at TWO DISTINCT HEIGHTS (three lower, three upper — diagnostic for the genus), with linear yellow-orange basifixed anthers about 1.0 cm; and a filiform style with a small trifid stigma reaching roughly to the corona mouth. Colour uniform yellow: tepals about RHS Yellow Group 9A-12A (roughly RGB 252,205,42), corona a half-step deeper and warmer (roughly RGB 252,183,20), often with a faintly deeper orange-yellow flush on the everted rim. Scent sweet, light, strongest at midday. If pollinated and not deadheaded the ovary swells into a green then tan three-chambered loculicidal capsule about 2 cm, splitting to shed angular black seeds.

## Care

**Light.** Full sun to light shade. Target 6+ hours of direct sun while foliage is green — critically INCLUDING the 6 weeks AFTER the flower dies, which is when the bulb is actually being built. Indoors: an unobstructed south/southwest window (roughly 20,000-50,000 lux peak, DLI 12-20 mol/m2/day). Below about DLI 6 the leaves etiolate and lodge and the bulb undercharges. Two exceptions a naive implementation gets backwards: (a) during the cold requirement the bulb needs NO light at all — it is rooting in the dark and light is irrelevant until the shoot clears ~5 cm; (b) an OPEN flower lasts noticeably longer in shade/cool, so the optimal light for flower display and the optimal light for bulb recharge point in opposite directions. Reward the player for moving it into the light once the petals drop.

**Water.** Four distinct regimes, and the boundaries matter more than the amounts. (1) Rooting, autumn: moisten thoroughly once at planting, then keep just-damp — the medium should never dry out completely while white roots are extending, but standing water at this stage is the single most common bulb-killer. (2) Cold period: barely any — cold + wet + no transpiring leaf = rot. Check every 10-14 days, water only if the top 5 cm is dry. (3) Active growth through flowering: water thoroughly when the top 2-3 cm is dry, let it drain completely, never leave a saucer full. A 20 cm pot of three bulbs in leaf drinks roughly 200-400 ml every 2-4 days at 15 C. (4) Post-flowering recharge: this is when water matters MOST and every naive player stops — the bulb is bulking and drought here costs next year's flower. Then taper hard as the leaves yellow, and keep the bulb DRY through summer dormancy. A Mediterranean-climate geophyte: wet winter, bone-dry summer.

**Temperature.** The defining requirement is a cumulative cold period: 12-16 weeks at 2-9 C, optimum 4-7 C, measured at the bulb, not the air. This is an accumulator — partial chill gives a partial result (see notes), not a binary fail. Below 0 C at the bulb does not count as chilling and freezing a potted bulb solid can kill it, so an unheated garage/fridge (4-5 C) beats a freezer. Growth and flowering are best at 10-16 C; the flower holds 14-21 days at 10 C, 7-10 days at 16 C, and 3-5 days above 21 C. Sustained above 21 C with wet medium is the trigger condition for Fusarium basal rot (the pathogen's optimum is ~25 C). Ground-hardy roughly -25 C (USDA 3-8); a pot has no soil buffer, so treat pot-hardy as about -8 C. Summer dormant storage: 17-20 C dry is ideal — that warmth is what initiates NEXT year's flower inside the bulb. Above ~25 C in storage degrades the preformed bud.

**Humidity.** Effectively humidity-indifferent, which is unusual and worth encoding as such: 40-60% ambient is fine, 30% is fine. Never mist — the glaucous wax on the leaves sheds water anyway, and free water sitting in the leaf axils at the bulb neck plus warmth is exactly the Fusarium/Botrytis entry route. High humidity is only a problem in combination with warmth. There is no humidity-driven decline mode for this species; if your engine has a humidity axis, this plant should be the one that ignores it.

**Soil.** Sharply free-draining, gritty, low-organic. A workable mix: 50% loam or soil-based compost, 30% coarse grit or perlite (2-5 mm), 20% leaf mould or coir. pH 6.0-7.0. Heavy wet clay is fatal over a season. Planting depth is 2-3x the bulb's own height — for a 6 cm bulb that is 12-18 cm of medium ABOVE the nose, so the container must be at least 25 cm deep; too-shallow planting is a leading cause of blindness and of bulbs splitting into non-flowering offsets. Nose upward; the basal plate (the flat, scarred disc) goes down. Feed low-nitrogen, high-potassium (e.g. 5-10-20) at shoot emergence and again immediately after flowering; high nitrogen produces lush floppy leaves, fewer flowers, and markedly more basal rot. Bone meal alone is nutritionally insufficient (phosphorus only).

### What a real grower knows

The single most important fact for a delayed-consequence design: NEXT SPRING'S FLOWER IS ALREADY BUILT INSIDE THE BULB BY LATE SUMMER. Floral initiation happens in the dormant bulb at 17-20 C in July-August. A bulb bought in autumn will bloom in spring almost regardless of what the player does — year one is not a skill test. What the player's care buys is the flower TWO cycles out. Model the bulb as carrying a preformed-bud object plus a stored-reserve scalar across seasons, and consider surfacing that (a cutaway, a 'the bulb feels heavy/light' hint) so the mechanic is discoverable rather than merely cruel.

Other things a real grower knows:
- Do not cut, fold, knot, braid, or rubber-band the foliage after flowering. Leave it 6 weeks minimum; ideally until it yellows and pulls away from the neck with a gentle tug. Tying the leaves into the classic tidy knot cuts light interception and is a reliable way to get a blind bulb next year.
- Deadhead, but only the seedpod. Snap off the swollen green ovary just behind the flower; LEAVE the hollow scape standing — it is green and photosynthetic. Letting a capsule ripen diverts a real fraction of the bulb's reserve into seed, and seed-grown daffodils take 5-7 years to flower.
- Daffodil roots are white, fleshy, and essentially unbranched, they grow ONCE per year in autumn, and they do not regenerate if broken. Handle and reposition bulbs before rooting, never after. A bulb whose roots were snapped in January is crippled for the whole season no matter how well it is watered.
- Contractile roots pull the bulb deeper each year. A bulb planted too shallow will visibly haul itself down over two or three seasons — a nice long-horizon detail if you track bulb depth.
- Ethylene destroys the preformed flower bud during dry storage. Bulbs stored near ripening fruit, or beside a Fusarium-infected bulb (infected bulbs emit ethylene), abort or blast their bud with NO external sign. An invisible, unappealable, fully delayed failure — the most honest possible version of this species' game role.
- Insufficient chill does not simply mean 'no flower'. Partial chill produces a short scape: the flower opens down among the leaves or barely clears them, sometimes still inside the leaf fan. That graded failure is far more readable than a binary.
- Blindness (leaves, no flower) has six distinct real causes — too shallow, overcrowding from offsets, foliage removed early, too much shade, excess nitrogen, and drought during the post-flower recharge — plus insufficient chill. If your sim can attribute the cause, that is a whole diagnostic minigame.
- Lift and divide congested clumps every 3-5 years. A bulb splits into offsets; a dense clump of small noses makes lots of leaves and no flowers.
- Every part is toxic: lycorine, galantamine, and calcium oxalate raphides. Bulbs are periodically mistaken for onions with serious results, and the sap causes 'daffodil itch' in florists. The upside is that deer, squirrels, voles and rabbits leave them completely alone — unlike tulips. Worth a line of flavour text.
- Cut daffodil sap is toxic to other cut flowers. Condition daffodils alone in water for 12-24 hours, then arrange WITHOUT recutting the stems.
- Bulbs that were commercially forced (supermarket pots) are usually exhausted and rarely rebloom in a pot; the honest advice is to plant them out and wait two years.
- Reject any bulb that is soft at the basal plate, light for its size, or shows chocolate-brown staining at the base. Fusarium spreads bulb-to-bulb in storage.

## Decline

### Insufficient cold period (vernalization deficit) — bulb kept above ~9 C, or chilled for fewer than ~12 weeks

Graded, not binary, and this is the whole point of the species. FULL FAILURE (0-4 weeks chill): 3-5 leaves emerge normally and reach near-full length; no scape appears at all. The pot reads as a healthy clump of grassy blue-green straps and nothing else — no bud, no stem, nothing visibly wrong. PARTIAL FAILURE (6-11 weeks chill): a scape emerges but stops at 8-15 cm instead of 35-45 cm, so the flower opens DOWN INSIDE the leaf fan, tepals pressed and creased against the surrounding leaves, sometimes never fully reflexing because the leaves physically block them. Corona often shorter than normal (2-3 cm rather than 4). SEVERE PARTIAL (4-6 weeks): a short scape with a bud that swells, the tan spathe splits partway, and the bud dries in place — tepal tips visible as a papery pale-yellow cone protruding 1 cm from the split spathe, then browning from the tips back over 3-4 days. Scape height should be a near-linear function of accumulated chill hours between the thresholds.

*Onset:* Fully invisible until emergence, then visible over the 10-20 real days of leaf and scape extension — i.e. the mistake was made 3-4 months before any symptom exists. In a 28-day game year: committed during the winter week, revealed on days 2-5 of the spring week.  
*Reversible:* Not for this cycle — once temperature-driven stem elongation has failed, no amount of care recovers it. No permanent mark on the bulb, though: a blind-from-cold bulb that is otherwise well fed and given proper chill the following winter flowers normally. Chilling can also be topped up BEFORE emergence — a bulb pulled at 8 weeks and returned to cold before the shoot exceeds 5 cm resumes accumulating.

### Foliage cut, mown, tied, braided, or folded down before natural senescence

NOTHING happens this season — that is the trap. The player gets a tidier pot and no feedback whatsoever. The penalty renders one full cycle later: leaf count drops from 4-6 to 2-3 per nose; leaf width drops from 1.5-2.0 cm to 0.6-1.0 cm, so the fan reads visibly thinner and more grass-like; leaf length drops from 40 cm to 15-25 cm; and either no scape forms, or a scape of 15-25 cm carries an undersized flower — perianth 5-6 cm across instead of 9-10 cm, corona 2-2.5 cm instead of 4-5, with tepals narrower and slightly translucent, showing gaps between them instead of overlapping. If your model exposes the bulb: circumference down 20-35%, tunic loose and wrinkled rather than taut, neck soft. Cutting at 2 weeks post-flower is roughly twice as damaging as cutting at 4 weeks — make it a continuous function of the green-leaf-days integral, not a flag.

*Onset:* Zero visible symptoms for the remainder of the cycle. Full expression at the next emergence — ~250-300 real days, or one full game year (28 days) later.  
*Reversible:* Recoverable, slowly. Left completely alone, a depleted bulb rebuilds over 2-3 cycles and returns to full flower. No permanent scar. But the lost season cannot be bought back within itself, and repeating the mistake compounds — three consecutive cycles of early cutting typically ends in a bulb too small to ever flower again without lifting and growing on.

### Ethylene exposure during dry summer dormancy — bulb stored in a drawer with ripening fruit, or next to a Fusarium-infected bulb

The most invisible failure this species has, and the most thematically perfect. The bulb looks and feels completely normal — firm, taut tunic, no discolouration, correct weight. Inside, the preformed flower bud is necrotic. Next cycle the bulb roots normally, chills normally, produces a FULL, healthy, correctly-sized set of 4-6 broad leaves, and either sends up no scape at all or sends up a scape bearing a hard, dry, brown, thumbnail-sized lump inside a spathe that never splits — 'bud blast'. Render that lump as a shrunken, mummified bud, matte dark brown, roughly 1 cm, held on a scape of otherwise normal 30-40 cm height. Healthy leaves plus a dead bud on a full-height stem is the diagnostic silhouette, and it distinguishes ethylene damage from cold-deficit blindness (which gives a SHORT or absent scape).

*Onset:* Damage is done in hours to days during storage. Zero symptoms for 4-8 months. Revealed at flowering time of the following cycle.  
*Reversible:* Irreversible for that cycle — the bud is dead tissue and cannot regrow. The bulb itself is undamaged and will initiate a fresh bud in the following summer, so it recovers fully in one cycle if the player identifies the cause. Which they cannot, without a hint.

### Underwatering during active growth (emergence through flowering)

Runs tip-downward, and starts on the OUTER, longest leaves. Stage 1 (mild): the distal 3-6 cm of the two or three longest outer leaves lose turgor and the leaf's shallow V-keel flattens out there; the tip folds over at a single discrete point rather than curving smoothly, and hangs over the pot rim. The crown and the inner young leaves stay upright — this contrast is the readable signature. Stage 2: the fold point migrates basipetally; the folded portion goes from glaucous blue-green to a dull grey-green (the wax bloom looks duller because the surface has flattened), then straw-yellow, then dry tan-buff from the tip back, with an irregular, slightly darker brown line at the moving boundary. Stage 3: the scape softens just below the ovary so the flower nods well past its normal 60-90 degrees, hanging almost vertically; tepals lose their flat plane, curl backward along the midline and become slightly translucent at the edges; the corona rim loses its crisp frill and folds inward. Stage 4: leaves collapse radially, splayed flat over the rim like a star, still attached and still partly green at the base.

*Onset:* 3-6 days without water at 15-18 C in a 20 cm pot; 2-3 days above 21 C. Stage 1 to Stage 3 in a further 2-4 days.  
*Reversible:* Turgor recovers within 2-6 hours of a thorough watering if no tissue has browned — the fold points lift and re-cantilever, and the flower re-orients. Any leaf tissue that has gone straw-tan is dead and never regreens; it stays as a browned tip for the rest of the season, so repeated mild drought accumulates a visible history of browned tips. A wilted-then-recovered flower keeps a permanent crease across the tepals.

### Drought during the post-flowering recharge (the 6 weeks after the petals drop)

Immediate symptom is mild and easy to dismiss: the leaves go limp and the fan splays outward over the rim, tips browning, and they yellow and die back 2-3 weeks EARLY — a premature, uniform, slightly grey-tinged yellowing rather than the rich buttery-gold of true senescence, with the leaves collapsing while still partly green at the base instead of drying upright and separating cleanly. The real symptom is the next cycle: a smaller flower on a shorter scape (25-30 cm instead of 40), and in a mature clump a proportion of noses go blind entirely — so you get a clump where two fans flower and three do not, an uneven, patchy result that reads very differently from total failure.

*Onset:* Foliage symptom in 4-8 days. Early die-back within 2 weeks. Real consequence one full cycle later.  
*Reversible:* The current cycle's leaves, once collapsed, do not stand back up. The bulb recovers over 1-2 cycles if watering resumes properly. Partial credit should be genuine: the recharge is an integral, so watering well for four of the six weeks gives you most of next year's flower.

### Overwatering / waterlogging, especially combined with warmth — Fusarium basal rot

Runs base-upward, the exact opposite of drought, and that directionality is the diagnostic the player must learn. Stage 1: the outermost leaf yellows FROM THE SHEATH UP — a yellow zone appears at the soil line and advances toward the tip, while the tip itself may still be green. Stage 2: affected leaves go soft and slightly water-soaked-translucent near the base, then flop from the base outward — the whole leaf tips over as a unit, pivoting at soil level, rather than folding at a point partway along. The fan opens out and lies over the rim in 1-2 days. Stage 3: an open flower goes limp and hangs; leaves pull free from the neck with almost no resistance, coming away wet and stringy. Bulb interior (if the model exposes it, and it should on death): the basal plate turns chocolate-brown and spongy, the rot advances upward between the fleshy scales as a wedge of brown, and a fine white-to-pale-pink mycelium appears between the scales. The bulb ends soft, dark, and foul-smelling, with the tunic sloughing. Roots turn brown, translucent and mushy.

*Onset:* 7-21 days at 18-25 C with saturated medium; can be 4-5 days above 25 C; very slow below 13 C. Latent infections from storage can sit dormant for months and then erupt when the medium warms.  
*Reversible:* Not reversible. Once the basal plate is brown, the bulb is dead — the meristem and the root-generating tissue are the tissue being destroyed. The correct action is discard, and discard the neighbours too, since the pathogen is soil-persistent and the infected bulb's ethylene damages adjacent bulbs' flower buds. This should be one of the few genuinely terminal outcomes in the game.

### Too warm while in flower (above ~21 C)

Everything runs fast and bleaches. The bud opens in 12-18 hours instead of 36-48. Within 48-72 hours the tepals go from saturated yellow to pale cream at the margins first, the fade advancing inward toward the midrib, and they thin visibly — the tepals become slightly translucent so backlighting shows the parallel veins. The corona rim browns: a fine tan-brown crisping starts on the frilled outer edge and works down, 1-3 mm at a time, and the rim's crispness collapses into a soft, papery, inward-curling flute. Anthers dehisce early and the pollen goes from bright orange-yellow to dull ochre. Whole flower is over in 3-5 days instead of 14-21. Separately, the scape may etiolate — thinner (0.5 cm instead of 0.9), longer (45-55 cm), and it lodges, bending at 15-25 cm from the base and putting the flower head on the substrate.

*Onset:* Visible within 24-48 hours; complete within 3-5 days.  
*Reversible:* The acceleration stops if it is cooled, but nothing recolours — bleached tepals and browned corona rim are permanent for that flower. No harm at all to the bulb, provided the foliage is then treated correctly. Purely a cosmetic/duration penalty, which makes it a good low-stakes teaching failure early in the game.

### Insufficient light during growth and recharge

Etiolation and lodging. Leaves overshoot in length (45-60 cm vs 35-45) while losing width and thickness, so the width-to-length ratio visibly changes; they go from glaucous blue-green to a soft, pale yellow-green, and — key shader detail — they LOSE THE WAX BLOOM, becoming slightly glossier and more saturated rather than matte and dusty. They cannot support themselves: the fan bends near the BASE (within the lower third) and splays flat, radiating over the rim in a floppy star, with the leaf blades twisting so alternate faces show. Scape likewise elongates, thins, and leans hard toward the light source, with the flower head pulled off-axis. Delayed component: the bulb undercharges, so the next cycle brings fewer, narrower leaves and a blind or undersized flower.

*Onset:* Etiolation visible over 7-14 days of low light. Lodging by 14-21 days. Next-cycle blindness one game year later.  
*Reversible:* Already-etiolated leaves never shorten, re-thicken, or regain the wax bloom — they stay floppy and pale for the rest of the season, so the pot carries the record. Moving it to good light immediately halts further elongation and the new growth is normal. Bulb recovers over 1-2 cycles.

### Watering through summer dormancy (the classic 'I should keep looking after it' mistake)

Absolutely nothing visible above ground, because there is nothing above ground — the pot is bare soil. The bulb below softens, the tunic stains dark and goes slimy rather than papery, and secondary rot organisms move in. The failure renders as a total no-show at the next emergence: the pot simply stays empty while every other plant in the collection comes up. If the player digs, the bulb is a brown, soft, hollow shell that collapses under thumb pressure.

*Onset:* 3-6 weeks of a consistently damp dormant pot at summer temperatures. No symptom until the following emergence window — 4-6 months.  
*Reversible:* Irreversible once the bulb has softened. Fully avoidable and fully recoverable up to the point of softening: a dormant bulb that has been damp but is still firm is fine if it is dried out. Give the player a way to check (lifting the bulb should be a legal action, with a firm/soft readout).

### Bud blast from root loss or too-rapid warming after chill (bulb moved from 5 C straight to 22 C, or roots broken during repotting)

The scape emerges and elongates to 60-75% of normal height, then stalls. The papery tan spathe swells and splits normally, exposing a bud that is the right shape but undersized and pale green-yellow rather than deepening to yellow. Over 3-5 days the bud stops enlarging, its tip yellows then browns, it loses turgor and creases longitudinally, and it dries into a stiff, papery, tan-brown husk still half-sheathed by the spathe — the two dry structures, spathe and dead bud, become nearly the same colour and read as a single brown claw at the stem tip. The leaves around it stay entirely healthy and green, which is what makes it look so wrong.

*Onset:* 3-7 days after the bud becomes visible; the causal insult was 2-6 weeks earlier.  
*Reversible:* Irreversible for that flower. The bulb is unharmed — it will flower next cycle if the foliage is now allowed to run its course. Prevention is the mechanic: warm bulbs up in stages (a week at 10-13 C between the fridge and the windowsill), and do not disturb a rooted bulb.

### Overcrowding — a clump left undivided for 5+ cycles

A slow, multi-cycle drift that should be almost imperceptible per cycle. Leaf count per pot goes UP while flower count goes DOWN: cycle 3 might show 3 fans and 3 flowers, cycle 6 shows 9 fans and 1 flower, cycle 8 shows 12 crowded fans and none. Individual leaves get progressively narrower (1.8 cm to 0.8 cm) and the fans emerge at visibly different angles and heights because the offset bulbs sit at different depths and tilts. The clump reads as a dense, congested tuft of grass. Surface soil heaves and cracks as the bulb mass expands, and small noses may push partly out of the medium, showing pale bulb shoulders above the soil line.

*Onset:* Detectable from cycle 4-5; unmistakable by cycle 7-8. This is the game's longest-horizon failure.  
*Reversible:* Fully reversible and satisfying to fix. Lift after the foliage dies back, separate the offsets by hand (they snap apart cleanly at the basal plate), replant the largest at correct depth and spacing. Full-size flowers return in 1 cycle from large offsets, 2-3 cycles from small ones. No permanent damage.

### Narcissus bulb fly (Merodon equestris) — a single larva hollowing the bulb during dormancy

Nothing during the dormant period except, if inspected, a small dark hole at the edge of the basal plate packed with brown frass. Next emergence produces the classic 'grassiness': instead of 4-6 broad straps, the bulb pushes up 10-20 very narrow leaves, 0.2-0.5 cm wide and only 10-20 cm long, in a limp, spreading tuft — a distorted mass of weak growth arising because the destroyed central bud has released a swarm of tiny lateral buds. Never any flower. Leaves are paler than normal and often twisted. Squeezing the bulb yields to thumb pressure and the neck is soft while the outer tunic still looks intact.

*Onset:* Egg laid in early summer; larva feeds through the dormant period. Symptoms appear at the next emergence, 6-9 months later.  
*Reversible:* Irreversible — the growing point is gone. Discard the bulb (and kill the larva). Preventable: firm the soil around the neck as the foliage dies back so the female cannot reach the bulb neck to lay.

### Hard freeze on an open flower or an advanced bud (below about -4 C)

Foliage is far tougher than players expect and should mostly shrug it off — expect only the distal 1-3 cm of leaf tips to go water-soaked and glassy dark green, then bleach to a pale grey-white and shrivel into a thin twisted thread, leaving a permanent white-tipped leaf. The flower is the casualty: tepals go translucent and glassy while frozen, then on thawing collapse into limp, water-soaked, slightly grey-brown tissue that dries to a thin brown film within 24-48 hours, still hanging from the stem. The corona browns and folds. A scape caught mid-elongation kinks at a single point and stays permanently bent even after recovery.

*Onset:* Damage during the freeze; full visual expression 12-48 hours after thaw.  
*Reversible:* Flower is lost permanently. Bleached leaf tips and the kinked scape are permanent for the season but purely cosmetic. The bulb is untouched — daffodils are ground-hardy to about -25 C — so this is a display loss, not a life-threatening one. Buds still low in the fan are protected and may open later.

## Lifecycle

1. 1. SUMMER DORMANCY / FLORAL INITIATION (real: 8-12 weeks, Jul-Sep). No above-ground parts; the pot is bare. Bulb firm, tunic dry and papery mid-brown, roots dead and shrivelled. Invisibly, at 17-20 C, the flower for the cycle AFTER NEXT is being differentiated inside the bulb. State that changes: preformed_bud initialised (or destroyed by ethylene/heat).
2. 2. ROOT EMERGENCE (real: 4-6 weeks, Oct-Nov, triggered by substrate falling below ~15 C). Still nothing above ground. White, unbranched, fleshy roots 1-2 mm thick push from the basal plate and reach 10-25 cm. This happens ONCE and is not repeated — broken roots are not replaced. State: root_mass 0 -> 1.
3. 3. COLD REQUIREMENT / VERNALIZATION (real: 12-16 weeks at 2-9 C, Nov-Feb). Above ground, at most a 1-3 cm blunt pale-green nose breaking the surface late in the period. This is a pure accumulator with no visual output — the hardest stage to make legible and the most important to the design. State: chill_units accumulate; scape_potential is set by the total.
4. 4. EMERGENCE (real: 10-18 days). A single tight spear of rolled leaves plus the enclosed scape pushes up inside a membranous cataphyll, blunt-tipped, pale whitish-green at first and darkening to blue-green as it clears the soil. 0 -> 10 cm.
5. 5. LEAF EXPANSION (real: 14-21 days). Leaves separate and unroll, extend to 30-45 cm, develop the shallow V-keel and the glaucous wax bloom, and arch outward 15-40 degrees from vertical. The lowest 3-6 cm stays pale whitish-green where it was sheathed.
6. 6. SCAPE ELONGATION AND BUD RISE (real: 10-14 days, overlapping stage 5). The two-edged hollow scape overtakes the leaves. The bud rides upright at the tip inside a green-then-tan spathe, then — the moment that reads most distinctively as 'daffodil' — the neck at the inferior ovary swings the bud over from vertical to 60-90 degrees while the spathe splits down one side and dries to papery tan.
7. 7. ANTHESIS (real: 24-48 hours to open, flower holds 7-21 days). Tepals peel back from the corona and flatten into a 6-pointed star; the corona extends and the rim flares and frills. Colour saturates to full yellow. Anthers dehisce over the first 2-3 days.
8. 8. FLORAL SENESCENCE (real: 5-10 days). Tepals fade from saturated yellow to cream, thin and go slightly translucent; the corona rim browns and crisps inward 1-3 mm at a time; tepals shrivel, curl, and hang as a limp brown skirt. The inferior ovary either swells into a green 3-chambered capsule (if pollinated and not deadheaded) or shrivels.
9. 9. FOLIAGE RECHARGE (real: 6-8 weeks, and non-negotiable). Leaves stay green, upright-to-arching, at full size. Nothing visibly changes for six weeks — the bulb roughly doubles in mass below ground and next-next cycle's reserves are laid down. The stage where the game's whole risk lives and nothing happens on screen.
10. 10. NATURAL DIE-BACK (real: 4-6 weeks). Yellowing begins at the leaf tips and along the margins and moves inward and basipetally with a ragged, irregular boundary; a warm buttery gold, then tan, then buff. Leaves simultaneously lose thickness, so they flatten, twist along their length, and sag progressively lower until they lie on the substrate. Finally they dry to straw and separate cleanly from the bulb neck with a light tug. Then back to stage 1.

**Onto a one-week season.** RECOMMENDED MAPPING: one game YEAR = four seasons = 28 real days, and this species uses all four. It is the wrong plant to squeeze into a single week, and squeezing it destroys the exact mechanic it exists to teach.

AUTUMN WEEK (days 1-7) — rooting. Bare pot the whole week. Player actions that matter: planting depth, one thorough watering, then restraint. Real 4-6 weeks compressed 5x.
WINTER WEEK (days 8-14) — the cold requirement. Bare pot, or a 1-3 cm nose by day 13. chill_units accumulate at roughly 1 real day = 2 real weeks of chilling, so the full 12-16 week requirement is met by about day 6 of 7 at 4-7 C. The player must simply LET THE WEEK ELAPSE at the right temperature. Nothing to do, nothing to see, and everything is decided.
SPRING WEEK (days 15-21) — the payoff. Day 15-16 emergence, 16-18 leaf expansion and scape rise, day 18 the gooseneck and spathe split, day 19 anthesis, days 19-21 the flower holds. Real 8-10 weeks compressed ~9x.
SUMMER WEEK (days 22-28) — the trap. Days 22-25 are the recharge: full green leaves, zero visible change, and the player must water, light, and NOT tidy. Days 25-27 die-back, tip-down yellowing. Day 28 dormancy begins and the pot goes bare again.

WHAT THE COMPRESSION HONESTLY DISTORTS, and what to do about each:

1. THE DORMANT:VISIBLE RATIO. In reality this plant is invisible or static for about 34 of 52 weeks. A linear 28-day compression still leaves roughly 14 of 28 days with an empty or unchanging pot. Do not fix this by shortening dormancy — the emptiness IS the species. Fix it by making dormancy an inspectable state (lift the bulb, feel firmness, see the tunic) so there is something to do that is not growth.

2. VERNALIZATION IS NOT A GROWTH STAGE. It is a counter, and it has no visual output at any speed. You can accelerate the RATE (2 real weeks of chill per real day) but you must not let the player skip the elapsed time, or the delayed consequence evaporates and the plant becomes an ordinary watering puzzle. Keep it a genuine accumulator with partial credit — partial chill gives a short scape, not a coin flip — because a graded outcome is the only way the player can reverse-engineer the rule.

3. THE SIX-WEEK RECHARGE COLLAPSES TO ~4 DAYS. This is the most damaging distortion, because six weeks of visible boredom is precisely what teaches a real gardener patience, and four days does not feel like a sacrifice. Compensate by making the recharge window the ONLY period when the pot demands frequent action (water, light position, deadheading) — substitute engagement for duration rather than shortening the penalty.

4. SENESCENCE COLLAPSES FROM 4-6 WEEKS TO ~2 DAYS. At that speed a naive implementation renders it as a global colour lerp, which reads as a health bar rather than a plant. Stage it explicitly even in 48 hours: tips and margins first with a ragged boundary, then blade-flattening and twisting, then sag, then straw and clean detachment. Four discrete visual events beat one smooth ramp.

5. FLOWER LONGEVITY IS NON-LINEAR IN TEMPERATURE. 14-21 days at 10 C but 3-5 days above 21 C — a factor of four, not a linear penalty. Under compression a per-day linear decrement will badly under-punish a warm room. Use a Q10-style rate multiplier on the senescence clock (roughly 2-2.5x per 10 C) rather than a flat modifier.

6. THE PREFORMED BUD BREAKS THE ONE-YEAR FRAME ENTIRELY. Floral initiation in summer sets the flower for the spring AFTER next, so the true consequence horizon is 18 months, or about 42 real days — one and a half game years. Do not compress this away. Carry two persistent state variables on the bulb across the whole save — preformed_bud (exists / aborted) and bulb_reserve (0-1) — and never reset either at a season boundary. That is the entire reason this species is in the roster.

7. IF YOU ARE FORCED INTO A SINGLE 7-DAY SEASON: compress to day 1 planting/rooting, days 2-3 chill (accumulator visible as frost on the pot rim), day 4 emergence, day 5 flowering, days 6-7 recharge and die-back. It works, but you lose the felt duration of both risky windows, and you must then double the mechanical severity of early foliage removal to compensate for the lost boredom. Say so in the design doc rather than pretending the plant is intact.

**The hard part.** 1. THE CORONA. It is not a cone and not a lofted cylinder. It is a tube arising from the fused base of the tepals — not from a separate receptacle disc — that flares only in its final fifth, then everts into a rim that is crenate with about six shallow lobes carrying a finer irregular ripple on top. The material read you are chasing is crepe paper, not moulded plastic: subtle longitudinal ribbing on the outside, visible venation, and a thin enough wall to translucate at the rim when backlit. Build the rim as a swept profile with two superimposed sine bands (6-lobe low frequency plus a 20-30 cycle high-frequency ripple at 10-20% amplitude) rather than a displacement texture, or it will read as noise.

2. THE ATTACHMENT TOPOLOGY. In Narcissus the six tepals fuse basally into a floral tube, and the corona arises FROM that tube. So the order along the axis is: hollow scape -> sharp bend -> green inferior ovary (1-1.5 cm, ovoid, three faint ridges) -> slender floral tube (1.5-2.5 cm, green grading to yellow) -> the point where six tepals radiate outward AND the corona projects forward, from the same ring. Naive builds put the corona on a flat receptacle with the tepals on a separate ring behind it; it looks subtly and unfixably wrong.

3. THE GOOSENECK. The flower hangs outward and slightly downward at 60-90 degrees, and the bend is a short sharp node at the ovary, not a smooth curve in the stem. Almost every reference photograph is taken face-on, which hides this completely, so models built from photographs come out facing straight up like a tulip. Get this one thing right and the silhouette is unmistakable from 30 metres.

4. WILT KINEMATICS. A turgid daffodil leaf is a keeled cantilever: the V-section provides the second moment of area, so it curves smoothly and holds its arc. Losing turgor destroys the keel first, and only then does the flattened section buckle at a single discrete point and hang as two near-straight segments. So the correct simulation order is keel_depth -> crease_point -> hang, and the crease must migrate basipetally over subsequent days. A uniform bend-along-the-whole-length interpolation — the default in almost every engine — is the most common single error in plant simulation and it will be the first thing a gardener notices.

5. DIRECTIONALITY OF YELLOWING. Drought and natural senescence run TIP-DOWN and margin-inward. Basal rot runs BASE-UP. If both use the same colour ramp with a flipped sign you have lost the single most useful diagnostic signal in the game. And true senescence is not a hue lerp: chlorophyll degrades and unmasks carotenoids that were already present, so the path goes green -> yellow-green -> warm buttery gold -> tan -> buff, passing through a saturated gold, whereas drought-kill goes green -> greyish straw -> buff without the gold. Two different gradients, and the leaf also thins and flattens and twists as it goes, so the geometry must animate alongside the colour.

6. THE GLAUCOUS SURFACE. The leaf is not a green diffuse surface. It carries an epicuticular wax that scatters short wavelengths, so it reads matte, dusty, and distinctly blue-grey at grazing incidence with almost no specular highlight. A standard green PBR leaf material makes it look like a generic houseplant. Model it as a thin scattering layer over the green base with view-dependent strength, make it fade with etiolation, and — for a detail almost nobody implements — let it rub off where the plant is handled, leaving darker green streaks that persist.

7. THE BOTTOM 5 CM. The base of each leaf, where it was sheathed inside the cataphyll, is pale whitish-green shading up to full blue-green over about 3-6 cm. It is a small thing that instantly makes the fan look like it grew out of a bulb rather than being planted into the ground as a mesh.

8. THE CLUMP. Offsets sit at different depths, different tilts, and different phases. Randomise per-fan origin (within a 3-5 cm radius), up-vector (up to 20 degrees), emergence timing (up to 5 days), and scale (0.6-1.0). A clump of identical fans from one point is the giveaway of a procedural system.

9. THE INTERIOR. The corona is open and the player will look into it. It needs: six stamens inserted in the tube AT TWO DIFFERENT HEIGHTS (three lower, three upper — this is diagnostic for the genus, not decorative), linear yellow-orange anthers ~1 cm, and a filiform style with a small trifid stigma reaching roughly to the corona mouth. An empty trumpet is very obviously empty.

10. MAKING AN INVISIBLE MECHANIC LEGIBLE. The genuinely hard design problem, not a modelling one: this species' two defining requirements — accumulated chill, and six weeks of pointless-looking green leaves — produce no visual signal at the time they matter, and their failure appears three to nine months later. Consider a soil cutaway view, a bulb-weight readout on lifting, and a chill accumulator surfaced as frost on the pot rim. Otherwise the player learns nothing from failing and just feels cheated.
